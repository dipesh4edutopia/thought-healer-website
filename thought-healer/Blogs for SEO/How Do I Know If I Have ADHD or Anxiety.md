# How Do I Know If I Have ADHD or Anxiety? Symptoms, Differences, and How to Tell

If you're struggling with concentration, forgetfulness, procrastination, restlessness, racing thoughts, or feeling overwhelmed, it can be difficult to know whether you're experiencing **ADHD, anxiety, or both**.

ADHD and anxiety can share several symptoms, including difficulty concentrating, restlessness, sleep problems, irritability, procrastination, and difficulty completing tasks. The difference often lies in **why the symptoms happen, how long they've been present, what triggers them, and whether they were present during childhood**.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition whose symptoms begin during childhood, even when the condition is not diagnosed until adulthood. Anxiety disorders, on the other hand, involve excessive fear, worry, or anxiety that is difficult to control and interferes with daily life.

Because the two conditions can overlap—and can occur together—it's not always possible to distinguish them through symptoms alone. A qualified healthcare or mental-health professional can assess your history and determine what may be contributing to your difficulties.

---

# ADHD vs. Anxiety: What's the Difference?

A useful starting point is to think about the **underlying pattern**.

### ADHD

ADHD primarily involves persistent difficulties with:

- attention
    
- organization
    
- impulse control
    
- activity regulation
    
- task management
    

The symptoms generally begin in childhood and continue into later life, although they may become more noticeable when adult responsibilities increase. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Anxiety

Anxiety disorders primarily involve excessive or persistent:

- worry
    
- fear
    
- nervousness
    
- apprehension
    
- physical arousal
    

The worry may focus on health, work, relationships, finances, safety, social situations, or many everyday possibilities.

Anxiety can also make it difficult to concentrate because your attention is occupied by worrying or anticipating something going wrong.

The important point is that **both ADHD and anxiety can make concentration difficult, but they may disrupt attention for different reasons**.

---

# What Symptoms Do ADHD and Anxiety Have in Common?

The overlap is one reason people often aren't sure which condition they may have.

Both can involve:

- difficulty concentrating
    
- forgetfulness
    
- restlessness
    
- irritability
    
- procrastination
    
- sleep difficulties
    
- feeling overwhelmed
    
- difficulty completing tasks
    
- racing thoughts
    
- problems with work or school
    
- physical tension
    
- difficulty relaxing
    

For example, imagine you're trying to complete an important report.

With ADHD, you might start the report, notice an unrelated notification, open another tab, remember another task, and lose track of the original assignment.

With anxiety, you might remain focused on the report but repeatedly think:

> “What if I make a mistake?”

> “What if my manager doesn't like it?”

> “What if I miss something important?”

Both situations can result in an unfinished report.

The **behavior looks similar**, but the underlying experience may be different.

# Signs That May Point More Toward ADHD

ADHD becomes more worth considering when you recognize a **longstanding pattern of attention, organization, and impulse-control difficulties**.

You may notice:

### 1. You've always been easily distracted

You may have struggled with attention since childhood or adolescence, even when you weren't particularly worried or stressed.

### 2. You frequently lose things

Keys, phones, documents, wallets, chargers, or other everyday objects may regularly disappear.

### 3. You struggle with organization

You may have difficulty keeping track of schedules, paperwork, projects, appointments, or household responsibilities.

### 4. You procrastinate even when you want to do something

You may know exactly what needs to be done but struggle to start until a deadline creates enough urgency.

### 5. You have difficulty estimating time

You may consistently underestimate how long activities will take or become surprised by how much time has passed.

### 6. You start many things but don't finish them

You may have plenty of ideas and intentions but struggle with follow-through.

### 7. You're frequently restless

Restlessness may involve fidgeting, moving around, feeling internally “driven,” or finding it difficult to sit through long activities.

### 8. You interrupt or act impulsively

You may speak before thinking, interrupt conversations, make spontaneous decisions, or have difficulty waiting.

### 9. These patterns existed before adulthood

This is particularly important.

ADHD symptoms are expected to have begun during childhood, even if nobody recognized them as ADHD at the time. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

# Signs That May Point More Toward Anxiety

Anxiety may be more likely when concentration problems occur primarily because your mind is occupied by **worry, fear, uncertainty, or perceived danger**.

You may notice:

### 1. Your thoughts revolve around “what if?”

Examples include:

- What if something goes wrong?
    
- What if I get sick?
    
- What if they don't like me?
    
- What if I make a mistake?
    
- What if I lose my job?
    
- What if something happens to my family?
    

### 2. You feel physically tense

Anxiety can produce physical symptoms such as:

- muscle tension
    
- increased heart rate
    
- sweating
    
- trembling
    
- stomach discomfort
    
- feeling on edge
    
- difficulty relaxing
    

### 3. Your concentration worsens when you're worried

You may be able to concentrate normally when you feel calm, but your attention deteriorates when you're anxious.

### 4. You avoid situations that make you anxious

You might avoid:

- social situations
    
- presentations
    
- driving
    
- medical appointments
    
- difficult conversations
    
- unfamiliar environments
    

### 5. You repeatedly seek reassurance

You may frequently ask others whether something is okay or repeatedly check information because uncertainty feels uncomfortable.

### 6. Your symptoms developed around a period of significant anxiety

If concentration problems appeared alongside persistent worry, fear, panic, or avoidance, anxiety may be contributing significantly.

# Is It ADHD If My Mind Is Always Racing?

Not necessarily.

“Racing thoughts” can happen with both ADHD and anxiety.

With ADHD, thoughts may feel:

- scattered
    
- rapidly changing
    
- easily distracted
    
- full of unrelated ideas
    
- difficult to keep organized
    

With anxiety, thoughts may feel:

- repetitive
    
- threatening
    
- focused on potential problems
    
- difficult to stop
    
- centered around uncertainty
    

For example:

**ADHD-like experience:**

> “I need to answer that email. Oh, I forgot to call the bank. I should clean the desk. Wait, I need to buy something. What was I doing?”

**Anxiety-like experience:**

> “What if I made a mistake in that email? What if they misunderstood me? What if they think I'm incompetent?”

The distinction isn't always this clear in real life, but noticing the **theme and direction of your thoughts** can provide useful information.

---

# Is Procrastination ADHD or Anxiety?

It can be either.

### Procrastination associated with ADHD

You may postpone something because:

- it feels boring
    
- it's difficult to initiate
    
- there are too many steps
    
- you're easily distracted
    
- the deadline feels too far away
    
- you struggle with time management
    

You might think:

> “I want to do this, but I just can't seem to get started.”

### Procrastination associated with anxiety

You may postpone something because:

- you're afraid of making a mistake
    
- you're worried about being judged
    
- the task feels threatening
    
- you want it to be perfect
    
- you're afraid of failure
    

You might think:

> “I know what I need to do, but I'm scared I'll do it wrong.”

Of course, these can overlap.

Someone with ADHD may procrastinate, become stressed about the deadline, and then develop anxiety about completing the task.

---

# Can ADHD Cause Anxiety?

ADHD and anxiety are separate conditions, but they can occur together.

For example, repeated difficulties with:

- missed deadlines
    
- forgotten responsibilities
    
- academic problems
    
- work difficulties
    
- relationship conflicts
    
- disorganization
    

may contribute to ongoing stress.

At the same time, someone can have an anxiety disorder independently of ADHD.

This is why it's important not to assume that anxiety symptoms are simply “part of ADHD.”

A professional evaluation can determine whether one condition, the other, or both may be present.

---

# Can Anxiety Look Like ADHD?

Yes.

Anxiety can interfere with:

- attention
    
- memory
    
- task completion
    
- sleep
    
- motivation
    
- decision-making
    

If your mind is constantly occupied with worry, you may have little mental capacity left for the task in front of you.

For example, you might read the same paragraph several times because you're thinking about an upcoming conversation.

That may look like an attention problem, but the primary driver could be anxiety.

The CDC recommends considering anxiety, depression, sleep problems, substance use, and other conditions when evaluating possible adult ADHD because they can produce similar symptoms. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/adhd-in-adults.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

# Can You Have Both ADHD and Anxiety?

Yes.

Having ADHD doesn't protect someone from developing anxiety, and having anxiety doesn't rule out ADHD.

When both are present, symptoms can reinforce each other.

For example:

**ADHD → missed deadline → stress → anxiety → more difficulty concentrating → further delay**

Or:

**Anxiety → poor sleep → reduced attention → more mistakes → increased anxiety**

This can create a cycle that makes it difficult to identify the original problem.

A proper assessment looks at the entire pattern rather than trying to force every symptom into one category.

# How Do I Know If My Concentration Problems Are ADHD?

Ask yourself a few broad questions.

### Did these difficulties exist when I was younger?

ADHD symptoms must have begun during childhood.

You may not remember obvious symptoms, but there may have been longstanding patterns involving:

- forgetfulness
    
- losing things
    
- daydreaming
    
- difficulty completing homework
    
- disorganization
    
- excessive talking
    
- impulsive behavior
    
- needing frequent reminders
    

You don't have to have been a “hyperactive child” to have ADHD.

### Do these problems occur when I'm not anxious?

If your concentration becomes poor primarily during periods of intense worry, anxiety may be contributing.

If similar attention problems occur even when you're calm, ADHD may be worth exploring.

### Do I struggle with organization even when I have no reason to worry?

Persistent organizational difficulties across different circumstances can be relevant to ADHD.

### Do my symptoms happen in multiple settings?

ADHD symptoms generally affect more than one area of life.

For example:

- work and home
    
- school and relationships
    
- work and social situations
    

### Have these problems significantly affected my life?

Think about:

- work performance
    
- academic performance
    
- relationships
    
- finances
    
- household responsibilities
    
- deadlines
    
- daily routines
    

The greater the functional impact, the more useful a professional assessment may be.

---

# What If I Can't Remember Having ADHD Symptoms as a Child?

Not remembering your childhood clearly doesn't automatically rule out ADHD.

A professional may explore:

- school reports
    
- childhood records
    
- family recollections
    
- old report cards
    
- behavioral patterns
    
- academic history
    

The goal isn't necessarily to find a childhood document that says “ADHD.”

Instead, clinicians may look for evidence of a longstanding pattern consistent with ADHD.

NIMH notes that an adult ADHD assessment may involve childhood reports or school records and information from family members or other people who know the individual well. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

# What If My Symptoms Only Started Recently?

This is an important distinction.

If you've never experienced significant attention or organization difficulties before and they suddenly appeared during adulthood, ADHD should not automatically be assumed.

Other possible contributors can include:

- anxiety
    
- depression
    
- chronic stress
    
- sleep problems
    
- medication effects
    
- alcohol or substance use
    
- other health conditions
    

ADHD is a developmental condition, so current diagnostic criteria require symptoms to have begun during childhood. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/adhd-in-adults.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

New or rapidly changing cognitive symptoms deserve appropriate medical evaluation.

---

# What About ADHD, Anxiety, and Depression Together?

It is possible for symptoms of all three to overlap.

For example:

### ADHD

You struggle to organize your work.

### Anxiety

You worry constantly about falling behind.

### Depression

After months of struggling, you feel exhausted, discouraged, and lose interest in activities.

This can make self-diagnosis extremely difficult.

Rather than asking:

> “Which one do I have?”

it may be more useful to ask:

> “What patterns am I experiencing, when did they begin, and how are they affecting my life?”

A professional can then determine which conditions may be relevant.

---

# How Is ADHD Diagnosed in Adults?

There is no single test that can determine whether an adult has ADHD.

Diagnosis involves a comprehensive assessment.

A clinician may ask about:

- current symptoms
    
- childhood behavior
    
- school history
    
- work functioning
    
- relationships
    
- organization
    
- time management
    
- impulsivity
    
- sleep
    
- anxiety
    
- depression
    
- medical conditions
    
- medications
    
- substance use
    

They may also use standardized questionnaires or rating scales.

Importantly, an ADHD assessment isn't simply a checklist.

The CDC states that diagnosing ADHD involves multiple steps and that other conditions, including anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, and learning difficulties, can produce similar symptoms. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/diagnosis/index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

# How Are Anxiety Disorders Diagnosed?

Anxiety diagnosis also involves looking at the **pattern, severity, duration, and impact** of symptoms.

A professional may explore:

- what you're worried about
    
- how often you worry
    
- whether you can control the worry
    
- physical symptoms
    
- avoidance
    
- panic symptoms
    
- sleep
    
- work and relationships
    
- other mental-health conditions
    

Different anxiety disorders have different symptom patterns.

For this reason, simply feeling anxious does not necessarily mean you have an anxiety disorder.

---

# Can an Online ADHD or Anxiety Quiz Tell Me Which One I Have?

Online questionnaires can sometimes help you recognize symptoms worth discussing with a professional.

However, they cannot reliably determine whether you have ADHD, anxiety, or both.

A quiz may identify symptoms without determining:

- when they began
    
- why they occur
    
- whether another condition explains them
    
- how they affect multiple areas of life
    

Use online questionnaires as **conversation starters**, not diagnoses.

# What Can I Do Right Now?

If you're unsure whether your difficulties are related to ADHD or anxiety, start tracking patterns rather than labels.

For one or two weeks, note:

### Concentration

When is concentration worst?

### Worry

What are you thinking about when you can't concentrate?

### Sleep

How much and how well are you sleeping?

### Tasks

Which tasks are easiest and hardest to start?

### Environment

Does concentration change depending on where you are?

### History

Can you identify similar patterns from childhood?

### Impact

How are the symptoms affecting work, relationships, school, or daily responsibilities?

This information can be extremely useful during a professional assessment.

# Practical Strategies While You Figure It Out

Regardless of whether ADHD, anxiety, or another issue is involved, certain strategies may help.

## Break large tasks into small steps

Instead of:

> “Finish the project.”

Try:

> “Open the document.”

Then:

> “Write the first heading.”

Reducing the starting barrier can make tasks easier to approach.

## Reduce unnecessary distractions

Try:

- turning off nonessential notifications
    
- keeping only necessary tabs open
    
- putting your phone away during focused work
    
- working in a quieter environment
    

## Externalize your memory

Use:

- calendars
    
- alarms
    
- written lists
    
- reminders
    
- visual schedules
    

Don't rely entirely on memory when an external system can do some of the work.

## Set a short work period

Instead of committing to several hours, start with a manageable period such as 10–20 minutes.

The goal is to begin, not to finish everything immediately.

## Address anxiety separately

If worry is consuming your attention, techniques such as slow breathing, grounding, structured problem-solving, regular sleep, physical activity, and talking with a mental-health professional may help.

# When Should I Seek Professional Help?

Consider speaking with a qualified healthcare or mental-health professional if:

- concentration problems are persistent
    
- anxiety is difficult to control
    
- symptoms interfere with work or education
    
- relationships are being affected
    
- procrastination is significantly impairing your life
    
- sleep problems are persistent
    
- you frequently feel overwhelmed
    
- symptoms have been present since childhood
    
- you're unsure whether ADHD, anxiety, depression, or another condition explains your symptoms
    

You don't need to be certain that you have ADHD or anxiety before asking for help.

You can simply explain what you're experiencing.

For example:

> “I've been struggling with concentration, organization, procrastination, and anxiety, and I'd like to understand what's causing it.”

That's enough to start the conversation.

---

# When Should I Seek Urgent Medical Attention?

Most ADHD and anxiety symptoms are not emergencies.

However, sudden severe confusion, a sudden major change in mental functioning, new weakness or numbness, difficulty speaking, a seizure, fainting, severe unusual headache, or a significant change in consciousness requires urgent medical attention.

These symptoms should not be assumed to be ADHD or anxiety.

# How Do I Know If I Have ADHD or Anxiety? The Bottom Line

ADHD and anxiety can look remarkably similar, especially when the main complaint is **“I can't concentrate.”**

A useful distinction is that ADHD generally involves a longstanding developmental pattern of difficulties with attention, organization, impulsivity, and/or hyperactivity that began during childhood. Anxiety is more strongly characterized by excessive or persistent worry, fear, apprehension, physical tension, and avoidance, depending on the specific anxiety disorder.

But there is no single symptom that can tell you which one you have.

You can also have **both ADHD and anxiety**.

If you're struggling with concentration, procrastination, restlessness, forgetfulness, racing thoughts, or overwhelm, look at the broader pattern: **when it began, what triggers it, what your thoughts are doing, where it happens, and how much it affects your life**.

A qualified professional can help distinguish ADHD from anxiety and other possible causes and determine what kind of support may be appropriate.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. How do I know if I have ADHD or anxiety?

Look at the overall pattern rather than one symptom. ADHD tends to involve longstanding difficulties with attention, organization, impulsivity, and/or hyperactivity that have roots in childhood. Anxiety is more often characterized by excessive worry, fear, apprehension, physical tension, or avoidance. Because symptoms overlap and the conditions can coexist, professional assessment is the most reliable way to distinguish them.

## 2. Can ADHD and anxiety happen together?

Yes. An adult can have both ADHD and an anxiety disorder. The conditions may also influence each other. For example, ADHD-related missed deadlines or disorganization may increase stress, while anxiety can further interfere with concentration. A professional evaluation can assess each condition separately rather than assuming that every symptom has one cause.

## 3. Does racing thoughts mean I have ADHD?

Not necessarily. Racing or rapidly moving thoughts can occur with ADHD, anxiety, stress, sleep deprivation, and other conditions. ADHD-related thoughts may feel scattered and easily pulled toward different ideas, while anxiety-related thoughts often involve repetitive worry, fear, or “what if” scenarios. The broader pattern and history are more informative than racing thoughts alone.

## 4. Is procrastination a sign of ADHD or anxiety?

Procrastination can occur with either. ADHD-related procrastination may involve difficulty initiating tasks, distraction, boredom, or poor time regulation. Anxiety-related procrastination may involve fear of failure, perfectionism, uncertainty, or avoidance. Because procrastination has many possible causes, it should not be used alone to diagnose either condition.

## 5. Can anxiety cause concentration problems?

Yes. Anxiety can significantly interfere with concentration because attention may become occupied by worry, fear, physical tension, or anticipating potential problems. Someone may appear distracted even though they are actually highly focused on anxious thoughts. Persistent concentration problems should be evaluated in context rather than automatically attributed to ADHD.

## 6. Can ADHD cause anxiety?

ADHD and anxiety are separate conditions, but they can coexist. Difficulties with organization, deadlines, impulsivity, work, or relationships may contribute to stress and anxiety for some people. However, anxiety should not automatically be considered a symptom of ADHD. A professional can determine whether anxiety represents a separate condition or is related to another circumstance.

## 7. Can ADHD start in adulthood?

ADHD symptoms are expected to begin during childhood. However, some people aren't diagnosed until adulthood because symptoms were overlooked, relatively mild, compensated for, or became more impairing as adult responsibilities increased. New attention problems that genuinely begin in adulthood should prompt consideration of other possible explanations. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 8. How is adult ADHD diagnosed?

Adult ADHD is diagnosed through a clinical assessment rather than a single test. A professional considers current symptoms, childhood history, impairment, functioning in different settings, and other possible explanations. Questionnaires, interviews, school records, and information from family members or others who know the person may contribute to the evaluation. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/adhd-what-you-need-to-know?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 9. Can poor sleep cause symptoms that look like ADHD?

Yes. Poor sleep can affect concentration, memory, alertness, mood, and task performance. Sleep problems can therefore resemble or worsen ADHD-like difficulties. Sleep should be considered when evaluating persistent attention problems, particularly if symptoms changed significantly after sleep became disrupted.

## 10. Should I get tested for ADHD or anxiety?

If symptoms are persistent or interfere with work, relationships, education, sleep, or everyday responsibilities, consider speaking with a qualified healthcare or mental-health professional. You don't need to know beforehand whether ADHD or anxiety is responsible. An assessment can consider both possibilities along with depression, sleep problems, medications, substance use, and other potential causes.

# Final Takeaway

If you're asking **“Do I have ADHD or anxiety?”**, the answer usually can't be found by matching yourself to one symptom.

Look at the bigger picture:

**When did the difficulties begin?**

**What happens when you're trying to concentrate?**

**Are you distracted by unrelated thoughts, or consumed by worry?**

**Do the difficulties occur even when you're calm?**

**Were similar patterns present during childhood?**

**Do they affect more than one area of your life?**

And importantly:

**Could both ADHD and anxiety be contributing?**

ADHD and anxiety can look similar from the outside, but they are not the same condition. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition with symptoms beginning in childhood, while anxiety disorders involve excessive or persistent anxiety and worry. Both can interfere with concentration and daily functioning, and both can occur in the same person.

If these difficulties are persistent or interfering with your life, speaking with a qualified healthcare or mental-health professional can help you understand what's actually happening rather than trying to diagnose yourself from an online checklist.
