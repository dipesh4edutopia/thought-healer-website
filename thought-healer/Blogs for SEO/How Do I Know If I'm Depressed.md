# How Do I Know If I'm Depressed? Signs, Symptoms, and When to Get Help

Feeling sad, tired, unmotivated, or overwhelmed from time to time is a normal part of life. But if these feelings persist, become difficult to manage, or start affecting your sleep, work, relationships, or ability to enjoy everyday life, you may wonder: **"How do I know if I'm depressed?"**

Depression is more than having a few bad days. It can cause ongoing changes in mood, thoughts, behavior, physical health, sleep, appetite, energy, and interest in activities. A healthcare professional can determine whether your symptoms meet the criteria for depression and whether another health condition could be contributing. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

## How Do I Know If I'm Depressed? A Direct Answer

You may be experiencing depression if you have persistent changes in your mood, interest, energy, sleep, appetite, concentration, or sense of self that last for around **two weeks or longer** and interfere with everyday life. Common signs include feeling persistently sad or empty, losing interest in things you usually enjoy, feeling hopeless or worthless, having low energy, struggling to concentrate, and experiencing changes in sleep or appetite. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

However, **you cannot diagnose depression from a checklist alone**. Similar symptoms can occur with grief, prolonged stress, anxiety, certain medications, thyroid problems, other medical conditions, or other mental-health conditions.

If you recognize several of these signs and they are affecting your life, talking with a healthcare professional is a good next step.

## What Is Depression?

Depression, sometimes called **major depressive disorder or clinical depression**, is a mental-health condition that can significantly affect how a person feels, thinks, behaves, and manages everyday activities.

Everyone experiences sadness or low mood sometimes. These feelings may happen after disappointment, conflict, loss, stress, or another difficult experience and may improve with time.

Depression is different because symptoms tend to be more persistent and can interfere with important areas of life such as work, school, relationships, sleep, eating, and self-care. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/depression))

Depression can affect anyone. It is not a sign of weakness, laziness, or a lack of willpower.

# What Are the Signs of Depression?

Depression does not look exactly the same in everyone.

Some people feel deeply sad. Others mainly feel numb, empty, irritable, exhausted, disconnected, or unable to enjoy anything.

You also do not need to experience every possible symptom to have a mental-health problem that deserves attention.

Common signs can be grouped into several areas.

## 1. Persistent sadness or low mood

You may notice that you feel:

- Sad much of the time
    
- Empty or emotionally numb
    
- Tearful
    
- Hopeless
    
- Discouraged
    
- Irritable or unusually frustrated
    
- Like things will not get better
    

Some people with depression do not describe themselves as "sad." They may instead say they feel emotionally flat, disconnected, or simply unable to feel much of anything.

---

## 2. Losing interest in things you usually enjoy

One of the most important signs of depression is **loss of interest or pleasure**.

You might stop enjoying:

- Hobbies
    
- Sports or exercise
    
- Music
    
- Socializing
    
- Spending time with family
    
- Dating or intimacy
    
- Food
    
- Travel
    
- Work you previously found meaningful
    

You may still do these things, but they no longer feel rewarding.

For example, you might think:

> "I know I used to enjoy this, but I just don't feel anything anymore."

This loss of interest can be particularly important when trying to understand whether a prolonged low mood may be depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

## 3. Feeling exhausted or lacking energy

Depression can affect your energy even when you have not done anything physically demanding.

You might:

- Feel tired most of the day
    
- Struggle to get out of bed
    
- Move or speak more slowly
    
- Find ordinary tasks exhausting
    
- Need more effort to complete simple responsibilities
    

This can sometimes be confusing because you may think:

**"I'm not doing enough, so why am I so tired?"**

Fatigue can be part of depression, and it is not necessarily a sign that you are lazy or unmotivated. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

At the same time, persistent fatigue can have many physical causes, so it is worth discussing with a healthcare professional.

---

# 4. Changes in Sleep

Depression can affect sleep in different ways.

You may:

- Have difficulty falling asleep
    
- Wake up during the night
    
- Wake earlier than usual
    
- Sleep much more than usual
    
- Still feel tired after sleeping
    

Some people spend more time in bed because they feel exhausted or want to escape from difficult thoughts and feelings.

Others struggle to sleep because their mind remains active or their mood is low.

Changes in sleep can occur with depression, anxiety, stress, medical conditions, and other circumstances, so sleep problems alone do not mean you are depressed. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

# 5. Changes in Appetite or Weight

Depression can affect eating habits.

Some people lose their appetite and eat less. Others eat more than usual, particularly when seeking comfort.

You may notice:

- Reduced appetite
    
- Increased appetite
    
- Unplanned weight loss
    
- Unplanned weight gain
    
- Less interest in food
    
- Changes in your usual eating routine
    

Significant or unexplained changes in weight should also be discussed with a healthcare professional because they can have many possible causes.

---

# 6. Difficulty Concentrating or Making Decisions

Depression can affect thinking and concentration.

You may find yourself:

- Losing your train of thought
    
- Forgetting things
    
- Struggling to focus on work
    
- Reading the same sentence repeatedly
    
- Taking much longer to make decisions
    
- Finding simple choices overwhelming
    
- Having difficulty completing tasks
    

This can sometimes lead people to worry that they are becoming incapable or "lazy."

But difficulty concentrating can be part of depression and other mental-health conditions. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

# 7. Feelings of Worthlessness, Guilt, or Failure

Depression can change how you view yourself.

You may experience thoughts such as:

- "I'm not good enough."
    
- "Everything is my fault."
    
- "I'm a burden."
    
- "I keep failing."
    
- "People would be better off without me."
    

These thoughts can feel extremely convincing when you are depressed.

However, feeling worthless does not mean that you are worthless. Depression can influence the way people interpret themselves, their circumstances, and their future.

Persistent guilt or worthlessness is one reason professional support can be valuable. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

# 8. Feeling Hopeless About the Future

Another common sign is losing hope that things can improve.

You might think:

- "Nothing is going to change."
    
- "There is no point trying."
    
- "Things will always feel this way."
    
- "I can't see a future for myself."
    

Feeling hopeless can make it harder to take steps toward getting help.

Importantly, **hopelessness is a symptom—not a prediction of your future**.

Depression is treatable, and support can make a meaningful difference.

---

# 9. Withdrawing From Other People

Depression can make social interaction feel exhausting.

You might:

- Stop replying to messages
    
- Avoid phone calls
    
- Cancel plans
    
- Spend more time alone
    
- Avoid family gatherings
    
- Feel disconnected from friends
    
- Stop participating in activities
    

Sometimes withdrawal happens because you do not want to burden anyone.

Other times, you may simply lack the energy or motivation to interact.

Becoming more isolated can then make low mood feel even worse.

---

# 10. Physical Symptoms of Depression

Depression can affect the body as well as the mind.

Possible physical symptoms include:

- Fatigue
    
- Changes in sleep
    
- Changes in appetite
    
- Changes in weight
    
- Headaches
    
- Body aches
    
- Digestive problems
    
- Muscle or other physical discomfort
    

NIMH notes that depression can involve physical aches and pains, headaches, cramps, and digestive problems. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

This is another reason depression should not be viewed simply as "being sad."

At the same time, physical symptoms can have many causes. They should not automatically be attributed to depression.

# Can You Be Depressed Without Feeling Sad?

**Yes.**

Depression does not always look like obvious sadness.

Some people primarily experience:

- Emotional numbness
    
- Loss of interest
    
- Irritability
    
- Exhaustion
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Withdrawal
    
- Sleep problems
    
- Changes in appetite
    
- A sense that everything feels like an effort
    

Some people may say:

**"I don't feel sad. I just don't feel like myself."**

That experience can still deserve attention.

Depression can affect people differently, and not everyone experiences every symptom. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

# Can Anxiety and Depression Happen Together?

Yes.

Anxiety and depression can occur together, and some symptoms overlap.

For example, a person may experience:

- Excessive worry
    
- Low mood
    
- Fatigue
    
- Sleep problems
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Irritability
    
- Physical tension
    
- Loss of interest
    

This overlap can make it difficult to determine what is happening based on symptoms alone.

A mental-health professional or doctor can assess the overall pattern rather than focusing on one symptom.

---

# How Is Depression Different From Feeling Sad?

Feeling sad is a normal human experience.

You might feel low after:

- A breakup
    
- Losing a job
    
- An argument
    
- Disappointment
    
- Failure
    
- Financial difficulties
    
- Grief
    
- Major life changes
    

These experiences can cause significant emotional pain without necessarily meaning you have depression.

A low mood may improve with time as circumstances change or as you process what happened.

Depression tends to involve more persistent symptoms that affect everyday functioning.

NIMH notes that, for major depression, symptoms occur most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, with either depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure being a key part of the pattern. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

That does **not** mean you should wait two weeks before asking for help.

If you are struggling, you can seek support sooner.

# How Do Doctors Know If Someone Has Depression?

There is no single blood test or scan that can confirm depression.

A healthcare professional usually looks at the overall pattern of symptoms and how they affect your life.

They may ask:

- When did your symptoms begin?
    
- How often do you feel this way?
    
- How long have the symptoms lasted?
    
- Have you lost interest in things you normally enjoy?
    
- How are you sleeping?
    
- Has your appetite changed?
    
- How is your energy?
    
- Are you having trouble concentrating?
    
- Are you able to work, study, or manage daily responsibilities?
    
- Have there been major changes or stressful events in your life?
    
- Have you experienced thoughts of death or self-harm?
    

A doctor may also consider physical causes that can produce symptoms similar to depression. Depending on the situation, they may perform an examination or order tests. For example, some medical conditions and medications can produce symptoms that overlap with depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

A questionnaire may also be used to help assess symptoms, but a questionnaire by itself does not establish a diagnosis.

# Can I Check Myself for Depression?

You can use a reputable screening questionnaire to help you think about your symptoms, but **a self-test cannot diagnose depression**.

A useful starting point is to ask yourself:

### Over the past couple of weeks:

- Have I frequently felt down, depressed, or hopeless?
    
- Have I lost interest or pleasure in things I normally enjoy?
    
- Have my sleep patterns changed?
    
- Has my appetite changed?
    
- Have I felt unusually tired or low in energy?
    
- Have I struggled to concentrate?
    
- Have I felt worthless or excessively guilty?
    
- Have I found everyday tasks unusually difficult?
    
- Have I become more withdrawn?
    
- Have I had thoughts about death, dying, or hurting myself?
    

If several of these questions resonate with you—especially if the symptoms are persistent or affecting your daily life—it may be worth talking with a healthcare professional.

# What If I Only Have a Few Symptoms?

You do not have to experience every symptom of depression before asking for help.

Depression can range from mild to severe, and people experience it differently.

NIMH notes that even people who have only some symptoms may benefit from treatment or professional support. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

You also do not need to wait until you are unable to function.

If something feels wrong and you have noticed a meaningful change in your mood, motivation, energy, or ability to enjoy life, that is enough reason to take your mental health seriously.

---

# When Should I Talk to a Professional?

Consider talking with a doctor or mental-health professional if:

- Low mood has lasted around two weeks or longer
    
- You have lost interest in things you normally enjoy
    
- Your symptoms are getting worse
    
- You are struggling to work or study
    
- Your relationships are being affected
    
- You are withdrawing from other people
    
- Sleep or appetite changes are significant
    
- You feel persistently hopeless or worthless
    
- You are struggling to complete normal daily activities
    
- You are unsure whether what you are experiencing is depression
    

You can start with a primary-care doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or qualified therapist.

A doctor can also help determine whether physical health issues could be contributing to your symptoms.

You do not have to figure out what is "wrong" before asking for help.

# What Can I Do If I Think I Might Be Depressed?

If your symptoms are mild and you are still able to manage your usual responsibilities, supportive habits may be useful alongside professional support when needed.

### Keep a basic routine

Try to maintain reasonably consistent times for getting up, eating, sleeping, and completing important tasks.

Depression can make daily structure difficult, but small routines can provide some stability. ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/tips-and-support/cope-with-depression/))

### Stay connected

Try not to completely isolate yourself.

You do not have to explain everything. A simple message such as "I've been having a difficult time lately" can be a starting point.

### Move your body

Gentle physical activity may support mental wellbeing. You do not need to begin with an intense workout.

A short walk, stretching, or another manageable activity can be a reasonable starting point.

### Eat regularly

Depression can disrupt appetite and routines. Try to maintain regular meals and adequate hydration where possible.

### Protect your sleep routine

Try to maintain a consistent sleep and wake schedule rather than spending increasingly long periods in bed.

### Do one manageable thing at a time

When depression makes everything feel overwhelming, reduce the size of the task.

Instead of:

**"I need to clean the whole house."**

Try:

**"I'll put away five things."**

Small steps still count.

### Talk to someone

A trusted friend, family member, doctor, therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist can help you avoid dealing with everything alone.

These strategies are supportive habits, not substitutes for professional care when depression is significant or persistent.

# What Treatments Are Available for Depression?

Depression is treatable.

Treatment can vary depending on the type and severity of depression and the person's circumstances.

Common approaches include:

- Psychotherapy or talk therapy
    
- Medication prescribed by an appropriate healthcare professional
    
- A combination of therapy and medication
    
- Other treatments for certain situations
    

Psychotherapy can help people develop new ways of thinking and behaving and address patterns that contribute to depression. Treatment decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare professional based on individual circumstances. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

You do not need to decide on a treatment yourself before seeking an assessment.

# When Depression Feels Like It Will Never End

One of the cruelest parts of depression can be the feeling that the current state is permanent.

You may think:

**"I've felt like this for so long that this must just be who I am."**

But depression can change how you see yourself and the future.

Feeling hopeless does not mean that improvement is impossible.

If symptoms have persisted or are interfering with your life, reaching out for professional support is worthwhile—even if part of you feels that nothing will help.

---

# What If I'm Not Sure Whether I'm Depressed?

You do not need certainty before seeking help.

You might be experiencing depression, anxiety, burnout, grief, chronic stress, a physical health problem, or some combination of these.

Some symptoms overlap considerably.

The goal of an assessment is not simply to give you a label. It is to understand what you are experiencing and determine what kind of support could help.

If you have noticed a significant and persistent change in yourself, talking with a healthcare professional can be a useful next step.

# When Should I Seek Urgent Help?

Thoughts about death, suicide, or self-harm deserve immediate attention.

If you are thinking about killing yourself, hurting yourself, or feel that you may not be able to keep yourself safe, **do not handle the situation alone**.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## How do I know if I'm depressed?

You may be experiencing depression if you have persistent changes such as low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness, fatigue, sleep or appetite changes, difficulty concentrating, or feelings of worthlessness that last around two weeks or longer and interfere with daily life. A healthcare professional can assess whether depression is present. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

## What are the first signs of depression?

Early signs may include losing interest in usual activities, feeling persistently low or irritable, having less energy, withdrawing from others, sleeping differently, experiencing appetite changes, or finding normal tasks harder than usual.

## Can you be depressed without feeling sad?

Yes. Some people experience depression mainly as emotional numbness, loss of interest, exhaustion, irritability, withdrawal, concentration problems, or a sense of disconnection rather than obvious sadness.

## How long do you have to feel depressed before it is considered depression?

For major depression, symptoms generally occur most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, with depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure being a key symptom. However, you can seek help before two weeks if you are struggling. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

## Can depression cause physical symptoms?

Yes. Depression can be associated with fatigue, sleep changes, appetite or weight changes, headaches, body aches, and digestive problems. Physical symptoms can also have other causes, so they should not automatically be attributed to depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

## Can anxiety and depression happen together?

Yes. Anxiety and depression can occur together, and they share some symptoms such as sleep problems, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and irritability. A professional assessment can help clarify what you are experiencing.

## Is feeling tired all the time a sign of depression?

Fatigue or low energy can be a symptom of depression, but tiredness has many possible causes, including sleep problems, physical health conditions, medications, and stress. Persistent fatigue deserves appropriate evaluation.

## Can depression make you lose interest in everything?

Depression can cause a significant loss of interest or pleasure in activities that you normally enjoy. This symptom is sometimes called anhedonia and is an important feature of depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

## Can depression go away on its own?

Some periods of low mood improve with time, but persistent or worsening depressive symptoms should not simply be ignored. Professional support can help, and effective treatments are available. ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/depression-in-adults/overview/))

## When should I see a doctor about depression?

Consider talking with a doctor or mental-health professional when symptoms persist, interfere with daily life, worsen, or make it difficult to work, study, maintain relationships, or care for yourself. Seek urgent help for suicidal thoughts or immediate safety concerns.

# The Bottom Line

So, **how do you know if you're depressed?**

Look beyond whether you feel sad on a particular day.

Pay attention to **patterns**.

If you have been experiencing persistent low mood, loss of interest, exhaustion, sleep or appetite changes, concentration problems, hopelessness, guilt, withdrawal, or other significant changes—and they are affecting your everyday life—it is worth talking with a healthcare professional.

You do not have to diagnose yourself.

And you do not have to wait until things become unbearable before asking for help.

Depression is a real health condition, and effective support and treatment are available.
