# What Should I Do If I Feel Like Hurting Myself?

If you feel like hurting yourself, **please treat the feeling seriously and do not face it alone**. You do not need to figure out everything about your life right now. The immediate goal is to get through the next few minutes safely and bring another person into the situation.

**If you think you may hurt yourself soon, have already seriously injured yourself, or have taken an overdose, get emergency medical help now. Go to the nearest emergency department or contact your local emergency service. If possible, stay with another person rather than being alone.**

If you're in India, **Tele-MANAS provides 24/7 mental-health support at 14416 or 1800-89-14416**. The Government of India says the service provides tele-mental-health support and can connect people with further specialist or in-person care when needed.

Feeling like hurting yourself can be frightening, but **having these thoughts does not mean you have to act on them**. Suicidal or self-harm thoughts are signs of serious emotional distress, and effective help is available.

# If You Feel Like Hurting Yourself Right Now

You don't have to solve the underlying problem right now.

Focus on **staying safe for the next few minutes**.

### 1. Get around another person

If possible, go to someone you trust.

You can say:

> **“I'm having thoughts about hurting myself and I don't feel safe being alone.”**

You don't need to explain everything.

If talking feels difficult, send that sentence as a text message.

The World Health Organization recommends reaching out to someone you trust and seeking help from a healthcare professional when experiencing suicidal thoughts.

### 2. Move away from things you could use to hurt yourself

If there are objects, medications, substances, or other means nearby that could be used for self-harm, create distance from them.

If possible, ask another person to hold onto medications or other potentially dangerous items while you're struggling.

Reducing access to potentially lethal means can create valuable time for the crisis to pass and for support to reach you.

### 3. Don't isolate yourself

Go somewhere where other people are present.

You could sit with:

- a family member
    
- a friend
    
- a roommate
    
- a trusted colleague
    
- a neighbor
    
- a healthcare professional
    

If you're already in immediate danger, don't rely on distraction alone. Seek urgent professional or emergency help.

### 4. Call for support

In India, you can call **Tele-MANAS at 14416 or 1800-89-14416**. It is a Government of India 24/7 tele-mental-health service.

[Government of India – Tele-MANAS information](https://www.dghs.mohfw.gov.in/national-mental-health-programme.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

If you're outside India, contact your country's crisis service or emergency service.

### 5. If you're in immediate danger, seek emergency care

Don't wait to see whether the feeling becomes stronger.

If you believe you may act on suicidal thoughts, cannot keep yourself safe, have seriously injured yourself, or have taken an overdose, **go to the nearest emergency department or contact emergency medical services now**.

A mental-health emergency deserves the same seriousness as a physical medical emergency.

# What Does It Mean to Feel Like Hurting Yourself?

“Feeling like hurting myself” can mean different things.

Someone might experience:

- an urge to physically hurt themselves
    
- thoughts about suicide
    
- thoughts about wanting to disappear
    
- feeling that they cannot tolerate emotional pain anymore
    
- thoughts that other people would be better off without them
    
- urges to punish themselves
    
- overwhelming anger directed toward themselves
    
- intrusive thoughts about self-harm that they don't actually want to act on
    

These experiences can occur for different reasons.

They may be associated with depression, anxiety, trauma, overwhelming stress, emotional dysregulation, substance use, relationship difficulties, or other mental-health conditions.

Sometimes people experience self-harm urges without wanting to die.

At other times, thoughts of self-harm and suicide can overlap.

**You do not need to determine which category you fall into before asking for help.**

---

# Having an Urge Does Not Mean You Have to Act on It

An urge can feel extremely powerful without being permanent.

When emotional distress becomes intense, the brain can become focused on escaping the immediate pain. This can make the situation feel as though there is no other option.

But a thought, image, feeling, or urge is not the same thing as an action.

You can have an urge and still choose to delay acting on it.

The immediate goal isn't necessarily to make the feeling disappear.

The goal is to **create enough safety and time to get support**.

---

# What Can I Do During a Strong Self-Harm Urge?

If you are not in immediate danger and are able to stay safe, a few short-term strategies may help you get through the peak of an urge.

## Delay acting

Tell yourself:

**“I don't have to act on this feeling right now.”**

Focus on getting through the next few minutes rather than deciding what to do about everything else in your life.

## Change your environment

Move to a different room.

Go somewhere other people are present.

If you're alone, contact someone and ask them to stay with you.

Changing your surroundings can interrupt the cycle of isolation and escalating distress.

## Use grounding

Bring your attention to what is happening around you.

For example, notice:

- what you can see
    
- what you can hear
    
- what your feet feel against the floor
    
- what your hands are touching
    
- the sensation of your breathing
    

The purpose isn't to pretend that everything is fine. It is to bring your attention back to the present moment.

## Contact someone

You don't have to wait until you're at your worst.

You can send:

> **“I'm having a really difficult time and I'm worried I might hurt myself. Can you stay with me?”**

Or simply:

> **“I don't feel safe being alone right now.”**

If the first person doesn't respond appropriately, contact someone else.

Keep reaching out.

WHO emphasizes that talking about suicidal thoughts and seeking support can help people through severe emotional distress.

# What If I'm Afraid to Tell Someone?

Many people worry that others will judge them, panic, become angry, or not understand.

You may also worry:

- “They'll think I'm attention-seeking.”
    
- “I'll become a burden.”
    
- “They won't take me seriously.”
    
- “My family will overreact.”
    
- “I don't want anyone to know.”
    
- “I should be able to handle this myself.”
    

But you don't have to handle a crisis alone.

You don't need to explain the entire history of what you're experiencing.

Start with the most important information:

**“I'm having thoughts about hurting myself, and I need someone to stay with me.”**

That is enough to start getting help.

---

# Should I Tell Someone If I Have Suicidal Thoughts?

Yes.

Talking about suicidal thoughts does **not** cause someone to become suicidal. Research summarized by NIMH indicates that directly asking someone about suicide does not increase suicidal behavior or thoughts.

If you're worried about yourself, tell someone.

If you're worried about another person, you can ask directly whether they are thinking about suicide.

You don't have to be a mental-health expert.

Your role is to help the person connect with safety and professional support.

---

# What If I Don't Actually Want to Die?

This is important.

Some people experience urges to self-harm without wanting to die.

Others may have thoughts such as:

- “I just want the emotional pain to stop.”
    
- “I want everything to shut off.”
    
- “I want someone to understand how much I'm hurting.”
    
- “I want to punish myself.”
    
- “I need a way to release this feeling.”
    

Even if you don't intend to die, **self-harm still deserves professional attention**.

You don't have to wait until you develop suicidal thoughts before asking for help.

A psychologist, psychiatrist, counselor, or other qualified mental-health professional can help you understand what is driving the urges and develop safer ways to cope with overwhelming emotions.

---

# When Is It an Emergency?

Treat the situation as an emergency if:

- you think you may hurt yourself soon
    
- you have made a suicide plan and feel you may act on it
    
- you have access to something you could use to seriously harm yourself
    
- you have already seriously injured yourself
    
- you have taken an overdose or potentially dangerous amount of medication or another substance
    
- you feel unable to control your actions
    
- you cannot keep yourself safe
    
- someone else is concerned that you are in immediate danger
    

In these situations, **don't stay alone and don't wait for the feeling to pass on its own**.

Seek emergency medical help.

If you're in India, Tele-MANAS can also provide 24/7 mental-health support at **14416 or 1800-89-14416**, but an immediate medical emergency should be taken directly to emergency services or the nearest emergency department.

# Warning Signs That a Crisis May Be Getting More Serious

Some changes can indicate that suicidal risk is increasing.

These may include:

- feeling increasingly hopeless
    
- believing there is no way out
    
- withdrawing from other people
    
- talking about being a burden
    
- talking about death or wanting to die
    
- making preparations for death
    
- giving away important belongings
    
- saying goodbye to people
    
- sudden major changes in mood
    
- increased alcohol or drug use
    
- increasingly risky behavior
    
- searching for ways to die
    

These signs don't predict exactly what someone will do, but they should be taken seriously, particularly when they are new or becoming more intense.

# What If Someone Tells Me They Want to Hurt Themselves?

If a friend, partner, family member, or colleague tells you they want to hurt themselves, take them seriously.

### Listen without judging

You don't need to immediately solve their problems.

Try to listen and understand what they're experiencing.

### Ask directly about suicide

If you're concerned they may be suicidal, you can ask:

**“Are you thinking about suicide?”**

Asking directly does not put the idea into their head.

### Don't leave them alone if there is immediate danger

If they appear to be at immediate risk, stay with them or make sure another trusted person stays with them while professional help is arranged.

### Help them connect with professional support

Offer to:

- call a mental-health service with them
    
- contact a doctor
    
- arrange a mental-health appointment
    
- accompany them to an emergency department
    
- contact a trusted family member
    

### Don't promise secrecy

If someone's life may be at risk, keeping the situation secret can make it harder to protect them.

Their safety is more important than keeping a promise of confidentiality between friends.

# Why Do People Feel Like Hurting Themselves?

There isn't one universal reason.

Self-harm or suicidal thoughts can occur when emotional distress becomes difficult to tolerate.

Possible contributing factors include:

### Depression

Depression can involve hopelessness, worthlessness, emotional pain, loss of interest, and thoughts of death or suicide.

### Anxiety

Severe anxiety can become exhausting and overwhelming, particularly when someone feels unable to escape the source of fear or worry.

### Trauma

Past or ongoing traumatic experiences can contribute to intense emotional distress and difficulty regulating emotions.

### Relationship problems

Breakups, conflict, rejection, loneliness, abuse, or feeling disconnected from others can contribute to emotional crises.

### Major life stress

Financial difficulties, academic pressure, work problems, bereavement, illness, or other major changes can overwhelm coping resources.

### Substance use

Alcohol and drugs can worsen impulsivity, mood instability, and judgment and can make a crisis more dangerous.

### Other mental-health conditions

Self-harm or suicidal thoughts can occur alongside several different mental-health conditions.

This is why a professional assessment is important.

---

# Does Feeling Like Hurting Myself Mean I Have Depression?

Not necessarily.

Self-harm or suicidal thoughts can occur in depression, but they can also occur with anxiety, trauma-related problems, severe stress, substance use, other mental-health conditions, or intense emotional distress.

A single symptom cannot establish a diagnosis.

If these thoughts are occurring, the more important question is:

**“How can I stay safe and get the support I need?”**

A qualified professional can then help determine whether an underlying mental-health condition is contributing to what you're experiencing.

---

# Can Therapy Help With Self-Harm Urges?

Yes.

Psychotherapy can help people understand the emotions, thoughts, behaviors, and circumstances connected to self-harm or suicidal thinking.

Treatment may focus on:

- managing overwhelming emotions
    
- identifying triggers
    
- developing healthier coping strategies
    
- improving relationships
    
- addressing depression or anxiety
    
- processing traumatic experiences
    
- reducing impulsive behavior
    
- developing a safety plan
    
- recognizing early warning signs
    

The exact treatment depends on the individual's situation.

You don't need to wait until you have stopped self-harming before beginning therapy.

---

# What Is a Safety Plan?

A safety plan is a personalized plan for what you will do when suicidal or self-harm thoughts become intense.

It can include:

1. **Your warning signs** — how you know a crisis may be starting.
    
2. **Things that help you get through the moment** — activities or grounding strategies.
    
3. **People you can contact** — friends, family, or trusted people.
    
4. **Professional contacts** — therapists, doctors, crisis services.
    
5. **Ways to make your environment safer** — reducing access to things you could use to seriously hurt yourself.
    
6. **Reasons to stay safe** — people, responsibilities, goals, values, or experiences that matter to you.
    

WHO recommends identifying warning signs, supportive people, professional contacts, and ways to make the environment safer as part of responding to suicidal crises.

A mental-health professional can help you create a safety plan that is appropriate for your circumstances.

# What Not to Do During a Crisis

When self-harm or suicidal thoughts are intense, try not to:

- isolate yourself
    
- use alcohol or drugs to numb the feelings
    
- make major irreversible decisions
    
- assume the feeling will last forever
    
- keep the situation secret if you are unsafe
    
- rely solely on online advice
    
- stop prescribed treatment without medical guidance
    
- dismiss the thoughts because “other people have it worse”
    

You deserve support even if you cannot explain why you feel this way.

---

# What If I Feel Better After the Crisis Passes?

Feeling better doesn't necessarily mean the underlying problem has been resolved.

A crisis can pass and then return.

If you've experienced suicidal or self-harm thoughts, consider arranging professional support even if you currently feel okay.

A therapist, psychologist, psychiatrist, doctor, or other qualified professional can help you understand what happened and reduce the likelihood of another crisis.

Follow-up support is an important part of suicide prevention.

# Common Myths About Self-Harm and Suicidal Thoughts

## Myth 1: “People who talk about suicide are just seeking attention.”

**Fact:** Talking about suicide should always be taken seriously. It can be an important signal that someone is experiencing severe emotional distress.

## Myth 2: “Asking someone if they are suicidal will make things worse.”

**Fact:** Asking directly about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior and can open an important conversation.

## Myth 3: “If I don't have a suicide plan, I don't need help.”

**Fact:** Self-harm urges and suicidal thoughts deserve attention even when there is no immediate plan.

## Myth 4: “I should be able to control these thoughts myself.”

**Fact:** Severe emotional distress can overwhelm your usual coping abilities. Reaching out for support is a strength, not a failure.

## Myth 5: “If the crisis passes, I don't need help anymore.”

**Fact:** A crisis can pass temporarily while the underlying causes remain. Follow-up care can help identify triggers and build longer-term support.

## Myth 6: “Self-harm always means someone wants to die.”

**Fact:** People self-harm for different reasons, and self-harm does not always involve an intention to die. However, self-harm can increase safety concerns and should be taken seriously.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. What should I do if I feel like hurting myself?

Tell someone you trust, avoid being alone if you feel unsafe, move away from anything you could use to hurt yourself, and seek professional support. If you think you may act soon or have already seriously harmed yourself, seek emergency medical care immediately.

## 2. What if I don't want to tell anyone?

It can feel frightening to tell someone, but staying completely alone during a crisis can make it harder to stay safe. You don't have to explain everything. Try saying, “I'm not feeling safe alone right now and I need someone with me.”

## 3. Can suicidal thoughts go away?

Yes, suicidal crises can change in intensity, and people can recover. However, you shouldn't rely on the thoughts disappearing on their own. Reaching out during the crisis and getting appropriate professional support can help you stay safe and address what is causing the distress.

## 4. What if I want to hurt myself but don't want to die?

Self-harm urges still deserve attention. Tell someone you trust and consider speaking with a mental-health professional. If you think you may seriously injure yourself or cannot stay safe, seek urgent medical help.

## 5. Should I go to the hospital if I feel suicidal?

If you think you may act on suicidal thoughts, cannot keep yourself safe, have seriously injured yourself, or have taken an overdose, seek emergency medical care. You do not need to wait until the situation becomes worse.

## 6. What is the Tele-MANAS number in India?

The Government of India lists **14416** and **1800-89-14416** for Tele-MANAS, a 24/7 tele-mental-health service. It can provide support and connect people with additional mental-health services when required.

## 7. Can therapy help if I keep wanting to hurt myself?

Yes. Therapy can help identify triggers, manage overwhelming emotions, develop safer coping strategies, and address underlying problems such as depression, anxiety, trauma, or relationship difficulties.

## 8. What should I say if I want to tell someone I'm suicidal?

You can keep it simple: **“I'm having thoughts about suicide and I'm worried about what I might do. I need you to stay with me and help me get support.”** You don't need to explain everything before asking for help.

## 9. What should I do if someone tells me they want to die?

Take them seriously, listen without judgment, ask directly about suicide if you're concerned, and help them connect with professional support. If they appear to be in immediate danger, don't leave them alone and seek emergency help.

## 10. Is feeling like hurting myself a sign that something is wrong with me?

It is a sign that you are experiencing significant distress and deserve support. It does not mean you are weak, broken, or beyond help. Suicidal thoughts are associated with severe emotional distress, and effective support and treatment are available.
