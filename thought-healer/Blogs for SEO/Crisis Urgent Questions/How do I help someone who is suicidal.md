# How Do I Help Someone Who Is Suicidal?

If someone tells you they are thinking about suicide, **take them seriously, stay with them if they may be in immediate danger, listen without judgment, and help them connect with professional support**.

You do not need to be a mental-health professional to help. One of the most important things you can do is stay present and help the person get through the immediate crisis safely.

If someone has a suicide plan, has access to something they could use to seriously hurt themselves, has already attempted suicide, or you believe they may act soon, **do not leave them alone**. Contact emergency medical services or take them to the nearest emergency department. The World Health Organization and National Institute of Mental Health recommend immediate action when someone may be at imminent risk.

If you're in India, **Tele-MANAS is available 24/7 at 14416 or 1800-89-14416** for mental-health support. For an immediate life-threatening emergency, use emergency medical services rather than relying only on a helpline.

Most importantly, **you don't have to solve the person's problems yourself**. Your role is to help them stay safe, feel less alone, and connect with appropriate help.

# What Should I Do If Someone Is Suicidal Right Now?

If someone has told you they want to die or you believe they may be at immediate risk, focus on **safety first**.

### 1. Stay with them

If you believe they may act on suicidal thoughts soon, don't leave them alone.

Stay physically with them when it is safe to do so, or arrange for another trusted person to stay with them.

WHO specifically recommends not leaving a person alone when they are at imminent risk of suicide.

### 2. Ask directly

You can ask:

> **“Are you thinking about suicide?”**

Or:

> **“Are you thinking about killing yourself?”**

Asking directly does **not** put the idea into someone's head. NIMH states that asking someone whether they are thinking about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior and can open an important conversation.

### 3. Listen

You don't need to immediately give advice.

Let them talk.

Try to understand what they're experiencing without criticizing, arguing, or minimizing their feelings.

### 4. Help make the environment safer

If it can be done safely, help reduce access to medications, weapons, or other potentially lethal means.

This is an important suicide-prevention strategy because creating distance from lethal means can provide time for the crisis to pass and for professional help to arrive.

### 5. Get professional help

Help the person contact:

- a psychiatrist
    
- psychologist
    
- doctor
    
- therapist
    
- crisis service
    
- emergency medical service
    

If there is immediate danger, don't wait for a routine appointment.

# The Five Steps to Help Someone Who Is Suicidal

NIMH summarizes helping someone experiencing suicidal thoughts into five practical steps:

1. **Ask**
    
2. **Be there**
    
3. **Help keep them safe**
    
4. **Help them connect**
    
5. **Follow up**
    

These steps provide a useful framework when you don't know what to do.

Let's look at each one in more detail.

---

# 1. Ask: Talk Directly About Suicide

One of the biggest fears people have is:

**“What if asking about suicide makes them think about it more?”**

Evidence does not support that concern.

If you are worried about someone, asking directly can make it easier for them to tell you what is happening.

You could say:

- “I've noticed you've been struggling. Are you thinking about suicide?”
    
- “When you say you don't want to be here anymore, are you thinking about killing yourself?”
    
- “Are you thinking about hurting yourself?”
    
- “Do you feel like you might do something to yourself?”
    

Ask calmly.

You don't need to sound perfect.

The goal is to open the conversation.

---

# What If They Say Yes?

Don't panic.

A person's answer doesn't mean you have to solve everything immediately.

You can say:

> **“Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you told me. I'm going to stay with you and help you get through this.”**

Then focus on safety.

You can ask whether they are in immediate danger and whether they have a plan or access to something they could use to hurt themselves.

If the person has a specific plan, access to lethal means, has already attempted suicide, or seems likely to act soon, treat the situation as an emergency.

**Do not leave them alone.**

Contact emergency services or take them to the nearest emergency department.

---

# 2. Be There: Listen Without Judging

Sometimes the most helpful thing you can do is listen.

You don't have to immediately say:

- “Everything will be fine.”
    
- “You have so much to live for.”
    
- “Think about your family.”
    
- “Other people have it worse.”
    
- “You shouldn't think like that.”
    
- “You need to be strong.”
    

Instead, try:

- “I'm here with you.”
    
- “I can see that you're hurting.”
    
- “You don't have to go through this alone.”
    
- “Thank you for telling me.”
    
- “I want to understand what you're going through.”
    
- “Let's get some help together.”
    

NIMH recommends listening without judgment and learning what the person is thinking and feeling.

You don't need to agree with everything the person says.

You can validate their **pain** without agreeing that suicide is the solution.

For example:

> “I understand that the pain feels unbearable right now. I don't want you to face it alone.”

---

# What Does “Listen Without Judgment” Mean?

It doesn't mean agreeing with every belief the person has.

It means avoiding criticism, shame, blame, or arguments while they are in distress.

For example:

### Instead of:

> “How could you do this to your family?”

Try:

> “It sounds like you're carrying an enormous amount of pain.”

### Instead of:

> “You have everything. Why would you want to die?”

Try:

> “I may not fully understand what you're experiencing, but I want to listen.”

### Instead of:

> “Stop thinking negatively.”

Try:

> “Let's focus on getting you through tonight safely.”

The objective is connection, not winning an argument.

---

# 3. Help Keep Them Safe

If someone is actively suicidal, safety becomes the priority.

Depending on the circumstances, this may involve:

- staying with them
    
- asking another trusted person to join you
    
- helping them move away from dangerous situations
    
- reducing access to potentially lethal means
    
- contacting a mental-health professional
    
- contacting emergency services
    
- taking them to an emergency department
    

NIMH specifically recommends helping reduce access to highly lethal items or places when someone is suicidal.

If you aren't sure what to do, it is better to involve professional help than to try to manage a serious crisis by yourself.

---

# Should I Ask If They Have a Plan?

If you're concerned about immediate risk, asking directly about suicide and whether there is a plan can help determine how urgently you need to act.

For example:

> “Have you been thinking about how you would do it?”

> “Do you have access to anything you could use to hurt yourself?”

You are not giving someone ideas by asking these questions.

You're trying to understand whether they are in immediate danger.

If they have a plan and access to the means to carry it out, take the situation extremely seriously and seek emergency professional help.

---

# 4. Help Them Connect With Professional Support

You don't have to become their therapist.

Your role can be to help them reach someone who is trained to provide appropriate care.

Offer practical help.

For example:

- “Would you like me to call a doctor with you?”
    
- “I can sit with you while you contact a therapist.”
    
- “I'll go with you to the hospital.”
    
- “Let's call a mental-health helpline together.”
    
- “Is there someone else you trust whom we should contact?”
    

NIMH recommends helping a person connect with trusted people, mental-health professionals, and crisis resources.

---

# What If They Refuse Professional Help?

This depends on the level of risk.

If someone is **not in immediate danger**, you can encourage them gently:

> “You don't have to figure everything out today. Would you be willing to talk to a mental-health professional with me?”

You can offer to make the appointment or accompany them.

But if you believe they are in **immediate danger**, don't treat their refusal as the end of the conversation.

Contact emergency services or seek urgent professional assistance.

Safety takes priority.

---

# 5. Follow Up After the Crisis

Support shouldn't necessarily end once the immediate crisis has passed.

Check in.

Send a message.

Call them.

Ask how they're doing.

Help them attend appointments.

NIMH recommends continued supportive contact after a suicidal crisis because ongoing connection can play an important role in suicide prevention.

You might say:

> “I've been thinking about you. How are you feeling today?”

or:

> “How did your appointment go?”

or simply:

> “I'm here if you need someone to talk to.”

You don't need to constantly monitor them.

Consistent, caring contact can matter.

# Warning Signs Someone May Be Suicidal

There isn't always an obvious warning sign.

Some people openly talk about suicide. Others become withdrawn or behave differently.

Potential warning signs include:

- talking about wanting to die
    
- saying they have no reason to live
    
- saying they are a burden
    
- expressing hopelessness
    
- feeling trapped
    
- experiencing unbearable emotional pain
    
- withdrawing from friends or family
    
- suddenly saying goodbye to people
    
- giving away important possessions
    
- researching ways to die
    
- making a suicide plan
    
- taking unusual risks
    
- increased alcohol or drug use
    
- major changes in mood
    
- significant changes in sleep or eating
    

NIMH recommends taking these warning signs particularly seriously when they are new or have recently increased.

One warning sign by itself does not tell you exactly what someone is thinking.

But if you are concerned, **don't ignore the change**.

# What Should I Say to Someone Who Is Suicidal?

There is no perfect sentence.

What matters most is that you communicate concern, listen, and help the person connect with safety.

### Helpful things to say

> “I'm really glad you told me.”

> “I'm here with you.”

> “You don't have to handle this alone.”

> “Are you thinking about suicide?”

> “Let's get some help together.”

> “Would you like me to stay with you?”

> “We can take this one step at a time.”

> “Your safety matters to me.”

---

# What Should I Avoid Saying?

Try to avoid statements that create shame or pressure.

### Avoid:

> “You don't really mean that.”

### Instead:

> “I'm taking what you're saying seriously.”

---

### Avoid:

> “Think about how much your family loves you.”

### Instead:

> “I'm here with you, and I want to help you get through this.”

---

### Avoid:

> “You have so much to be grateful for.”

### Instead:

> “I can see that you're in a lot of pain.”

---

### Avoid:

> “Promise me you'll never do it.”

### Instead:

> “Let's work together on getting you somewhere safe and connected with professional help.”

WHO advises against judgment, criticism, threats, or forcing someone to make promises about not harming themselves.

---

# Should I Promise to Keep Their Suicidal Thoughts Secret?

**No.**

If someone tells you they are suicidal, you should not promise absolute secrecy.

You may want to protect their privacy, but if their life is at risk, involving another trusted person or professional may be necessary.

You can say:

> “I care about you too much to keep this completely secret if I think you're in danger. I'll involve the people who can help keep you safe.”

NIMH and WHO both advise against promising secrecy when someone is at risk of suicide.

---

# What If They Say, “Don't Tell Anyone”?

If there is no immediate danger, you can discuss who they would feel comfortable involving.

For example:

> “Who would you feel safest having with us?”

But if you believe the person is at **imminent risk**, protecting their safety takes priority over keeping the information private.

Contact emergency services or an appropriate healthcare professional.

---

# What If the Person Is Angry With Me?

They may become angry when you involve other people.

That can be difficult.

You might hear:

- “You betrayed me.”
    
- “I told you not to tell anyone.”
    
- “You don't understand.”
    
- “Leave me alone.”
    

Try not to interpret the anger as proof that you did the wrong thing.

If you genuinely believe their life is at risk, getting help is more important than avoiding temporary conflict.

You can say:

> “I understand you're angry with me. I still care about you, and I couldn't ignore what you told me.”

---

# What If Someone Posts About Suicide on Social Media?

Take it seriously.

Don't assume they're “just looking for attention.”

If you know the person personally, contact them directly and ask whether they are safe.

If you believe there is immediate danger, contact emergency services or someone physically close to them.

You can also use the platform's suicide or self-harm reporting tools when appropriate.

The goal is to connect the person with real-world support rather than simply responding publicly to the post.

---

# What If the Person Has Already Attempted Suicide?

If someone has already attempted suicide or seriously injured themselves, **seek emergency medical attention immediately**.

Don't assume that because they are awake or say they're fine, the danger has passed.

If medication, drugs, or another potentially harmful substance was involved, seek urgent medical advice rather than waiting for symptoms.

If the person is unconscious, having difficulty breathing, severely injured, or otherwise medically unstable, contact emergency medical services immediately.

After the immediate medical crisis, ensure they receive appropriate mental-health follow-up.

---

# How Can I Help Someone Who Has Self-Harm Urges but Isn't Suicidal?

Self-harm and suicidal behavior are not identical, but both deserve attention.

If someone says they want to hurt themselves but do not want to die:

- listen without judgment
    
- ask what they are experiencing
    
- help them identify triggers
    
- encourage professional support
    
- help them stay around supportive people
    
- help reduce access to things they could use to seriously injure themselves
    
- develop a safety plan with a qualified professional
    

Don't assume that someone is safe simply because they deny wanting to die.

Self-harm can still involve serious injury and may coexist with suicidal thoughts.

---

# Can I Help Someone Without Being a Mental-Health Professional?

Yes.

In fact, friends and family members are often among the first people to notice changes in someone's behavior.

You can help by:

- noticing warning signs
    
- asking directly
    
- listening
    
- staying with them during an immediate crisis
    
- helping create a safer environment
    
- connecting them with professional care
    
- following up afterward
    

You **cannot and should not be expected to provide treatment yourself**.

Your job isn't to diagnose them.

Your job is to help them get safely connected to people who can.

---

# What If I Am the Only Person Who Knows?

This can feel overwhelming.

Remember:

**You don't have to carry this responsibility alone.**

If someone has told you they're suicidal, consider involving another trusted person and professional support.

If they are in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

You can say:

> “I'm really worried about you, and I need help making sure you're safe.”

Getting additional support is not betraying the person.

It is part of helping them.

---

# How Do I Encourage Someone to See a Therapist or Psychiatrist?

Avoid making it sound like an accusation.

Instead of:

> “You clearly need therapy.”

Try:

> “You've been dealing with a lot. Would you be willing to talk with someone who specializes in this?”

You can make the process easier by offering practical assistance:

- help find a professional
    
- sit with them while they make the call
    
- help arrange transportation
    
- accompany them to an appointment
    
- help them write down what they want to discuss
    

NIMH notes that mental-health treatment may involve psychotherapy, medication, or a combination depending on the individual's needs.

---

# When Is It Time to Call Emergency Services?

Seek emergency help if the person:

- says they are about to kill themselves
    
- has a suicide plan and appears likely to act
    
- has access to the means needed to carry out the plan
    
- has already attempted suicide
    
- has seriously injured themselves
    
- has taken an overdose
    
- is severely intoxicated and suicidal
    
- is severely confused or disconnected from reality
    
- cannot agree to remain safe while help is arranged
    
- is behaving in a way that makes you believe immediate action is necessary
    

In these circumstances:

**Do not leave them alone.**

Contact emergency medical services or take them to the nearest emergency department if it is safe to do so.

WHO recommends contacting emergency services, a crisis line, healthcare professional, or family member when someone is in immediate danger and not leaving the person alone.

# What About Your Own Mental Health?

Helping someone who is suicidal can be emotionally exhausting.

You may experience:

- fear
    
- guilt
    
- anger
    
- helplessness
    
- anxiety
    
- difficulty sleeping
    
- constant worry
    
- responsibility for the person's safety
    

Remember that you cannot control another person's actions.

You can offer support, encourage treatment, help during an emergency, and stay connected—but **you cannot single-handedly prevent every possible outcome**.

If you're struggling, speak with a mental-health professional yourself.

You deserve support too.

# Common Myths About Helping Someone Who Is Suicidal

## Myth 1: “Asking about suicide will make them suicidal.”

**Fact:** Asking directly about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior. It can create an opportunity for an honest conversation.

## Myth 2: “They are just trying to get attention.”

**Fact:** Any statement suggesting suicide should be taken seriously, particularly when it represents a new or increasing change in behavior.

## Myth 3: “If they promise not to do anything, they're safe.”

**Fact:** A promise alone should not replace appropriate assessment and support when someone may be at risk.

## Myth 4: “I have to solve their problems.”

**Fact:** You don't have to solve their life. Your role is to listen, help maintain safety, and connect them with appropriate care.

## Myth 5: “Talking about suicide makes the situation worse.”

**Fact:** Open, calm conversations can help someone feel understood and make it easier to connect them with support.

## Myth 6: “If they're laughing or acting normal, they can't be suicidal.”

**Fact:** People experiencing suicidal thoughts do not always appear visibly distressed. A person's outward appearance cannot reliably determine their level of risk.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. What should I do if someone tells me they want to die?

Take them seriously. Ask directly whether they are thinking about suicide, listen without judgment, and help them connect with professional support. If you believe they may act soon, don't leave them alone and seek emergency help.

## 2. Should I ask someone directly if they are suicidal?

Yes. Asking directly about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior. It can help someone feel understood and make it easier for them to talk honestly about what they're experiencing.

## 3. What should I say to someone who is suicidal?

Try saying, “I'm glad you told me,” “I'm here with you,” or “Let's get some help together.” Avoid judgment, guilt, threats, or minimizing statements. You don't need perfect words; calm presence and genuine concern matter.

## 4. Should I leave someone alone if they say they are suicidal?

If you believe they are at immediate risk, **no**. Stay with them and contact emergency or professional support. WHO recommends not leaving someone alone when they are at imminent risk of suicide.

## 5. Should I tell their family if they ask me not to?

If the person is at significant or immediate risk, safety takes priority over keeping the information secret. Involve an appropriate trusted person or professional who can help keep them safe. Do not promise absolute secrecy about suicidal thoughts.

## 6. What if they don't want professional help?

If there is no immediate danger, continue encouraging them and offer practical help such as accompanying them to an appointment. If you believe they are in immediate danger, seek emergency help even if they resist.

## 7. What if someone has already attempted suicide?

Treat it as a medical emergency. Seek immediate medical attention, particularly if there are injuries, overdose, poisoning, or altered consciousness. After immediate medical care, professional mental-health follow-up is important.

## 8. Can I help someone who doesn't have depression?

Yes. Suicidal thoughts can occur in the context of many different circumstances and mental-health difficulties, and not everyone who experiences them has a diagnosed mental-health disorder. What matters is taking the person's distress seriously and helping them access appropriate support.

## 9. How can I support someone after a suicidal crisis?

Continue checking in, encourage treatment, help with practical barriers to care, and maintain supportive contact. NIMH specifically recommends follow-up after a crisis because ongoing connection can be helpful.

## 10. What is the most important thing to remember when helping someone who is suicidal?

You don't have to solve everything. **Take them seriously, stay connected, help keep them safe, and connect them with professional help.** If they are in immediate danger, seek emergency assistance and don't leave them alone.

# Final Takeaway

If someone tells you they are suicidal, **believe them, listen to them, and take the situation seriously**.

You don't need to have the perfect words or know how to fix their life.

Start with five things:

**Ask. Listen. Stay. Make it safer. Connect them with help.**

If they may act on suicidal thoughts soon, have a plan and access to lethal means, have already attempted suicide, or cannot stay safe, **don't leave them alone—seek emergency help immediately**.

And remember: **supporting someone does not mean carrying the entire situation by yourself.** Involving family, friends, healthcare professionals, crisis services, and emergency care when necessary is part of helping—not a failure to help.
