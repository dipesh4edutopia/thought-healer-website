# Can Anxiety Cause Physical Symptoms? How Anxiety Affects Your Body

Yes, anxiety can cause real physical symptoms. When your brain detects a possible threat, your body's stress-response system can become activated. This may change your heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, digestion, sweating, and energy levels. Anxiety can therefore produce sensations such as a racing heart, chest tightness, dizziness, nausea, headaches, shaking, or shortness of breath.

But physical symptoms do **not automatically mean anxiety is the cause**. Many of these symptoms can occur for other physical or psychological reasons. New, severe, persistent, unusual, or concerning symptoms deserve appropriate medical attention.

## How Does Anxiety Cause Physical Symptoms?

Anxiety is not only something you experience in your thoughts. It can trigger genuine changes throughout the body.

Imagine that you are about to speak in front of a large group. A few minutes before you begin, you notice your heart pounding. Your palms become sweaty, your stomach feels unsettled, your shoulders tighten, and your breathing becomes faster.

You may think, "Why is my body doing this?"

Your body's alarm system is responding to what your brain perceives as a threat.

A simplified version of the process looks like this:

1. **Your brain perceives a possible threat.**
    
2. **Your stress-response system becomes activated.**
    
3. **Stress hormones and nervous-system changes prepare your body to respond.**
    
4. **Your heart rate and breathing may increase.**
    
5. **Your muscles may tense.**
    
6. **Digestion may change.**
    
7. **Sweating and other physical responses may increase.**
    
8. **As the perceived threat decreases, your body can gradually move back toward its usual state.**
    

The American Psychological Association describes anxiety as an emotional state that can involve physical tension, faster breathing, and a more rapid heartbeat. The body's stress response can also involve hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol.

This is why anxiety-related physical symptoms are **physically real**. They are not something a person is deliberately imagining or making up.

## What Physical Symptoms Can Anxiety Cause?

Anxiety can affect several body systems at the same time. Common anxiety-related physical symptoms include:

### Heart and circulation

Anxiety may cause:

- A racing heartbeat
    
- A pounding heartbeat
    
- Palpitations
    
- A stronger awareness of your heartbeat
    

During intense anxiety or a panic attack, these sensations can feel particularly dramatic. NIMH lists rapid or pounding heart rate among common physical symptoms of panic attacks.

However, anxiety is only one possible explanation for changes in heart rate or palpitations. Stress, caffeine, medications, heart-rhythm problems, and other conditions can also cause them.

### Breathing

Anxiety may be associated with:

- Faster breathing
    
- A feeling of shortness of breath
    
- Feeling unable to take a satisfying breath
    
- A tight feeling in the chest or throat
    

When you are anxious, your breathing pattern can change as your body prepares to respond to perceived danger.

But significant breathing difficulty should never automatically be dismissed as anxiety. Respiratory, cardiovascular, and other medical conditions can cause similar symptoms.

### Muscles and body tension

Anxiety and prolonged stress can contribute to:

- Muscle tension
    
- Tight shoulders
    
- Neck discomfort
    
- Jaw tension
    
- Trembling or shaking
    
- Muscle aches related to prolonged tension
    

Muscle tension is part of the body's protective response to stress. The APA notes that muscles can tense when the body is under stress.

### Digestive symptoms

Anxiety may affect the digestive system and contribute to:

- Nausea
    
- Stomach discomfort
    
- "Butterflies" in the stomach
    
- Indigestion
    
- Changes in appetite
    
- Constipation or diarrhea
    
- Bloating or stomach cramps
    

NHS guidance lists nausea, indigestion, stomach cramps, bloating, constipation, and diarrhea among possible physical symptoms of anxiety.

This is one reason people sometimes ask, **"Can anxiety make you feel sick?"** It can, but persistent stomach symptoms can have many other causes.

### Neurological and sensory sensations

Some people experiencing anxiety may notice:

- Dizziness
    
- Light-headedness
    
- Tingling
    
- Numbness sensations
    
- Headaches
    
- Feeling detached or unreal during intense anxiety
    

Panic attacks in particular can involve dizziness, tingling or numbness, headaches, and feelings of unreality or detachment.

These symptoms are not specific to anxiety, however. Dizziness, headaches, and sensory changes can have many possible causes.

### Skin and temperature

Anxiety can also be associated with:

- Sweating
    
- Feeling hot
    
- Flushing
    
- Chills
    
- Feeling unusually cold
    
- Shaking
    

These reactions are part of the body's broader stress response.

### Energy and sleep

Longer periods of anxiety may be associated with:

- Fatigue
    
- Difficulty falling asleep
    
- Restless or unsatisfying sleep
    
- Feeling physically drained
    

NIMH notes that anxiety can occur alongside fatigue and sleep difficulties, as well as headaches, muscle aches, stomachaches, sweating, light-headedness, and feeling out of breath.

## Can Anxiety Cause Chest Pain?

**Yes, anxiety can be associated with chest pain or chest tightness**, particularly during intense anxiety or panic. Panic attacks may include chest pain, rapid heartbeat, sweating, nausea, dizziness, and difficulty breathing.

But this is an important safety point:

**Do not assume new or unexplained chest pain is anxiety.**

Chest pain can have many causes, including potentially serious heart and other medical conditions. It can be difficult to distinguish anxiety-related chest discomfort from other causes based only on how the symptoms feel.

Seek urgent medical help for new, sudden, severe, or unexplained chest pain—especially when it occurs with severe breathing difficulty, fainting, or other concerning symptoms.

If you are unsure whether chest pain is related to anxiety, it is safer to seek medical evaluation rather than trying to diagnose the cause yourself.

---

## Can Anxiety Cause a Racing Heart?

**Yes. Anxiety can make your heart beat faster or feel as though it is pounding.**

When the body's stress response is activated, heart rate can increase as part of the body's preparation to respond to a perceived threat. Anxiety can also make you more aware of normal changes in your heartbeat.

This can feel like:

- Your heart is racing
    
- Your heart is pounding
    
- Your heartbeat feels unusually strong
    
- Your heart seems to flutter or skip
    

However, not every racing or irregular heartbeat is caused by anxiety. Palpitations can also occur for other reasons, including caffeine, medications, electrolyte problems, or abnormal heart rhythms.

## Can Anxiety Cause Dizziness?

**Yes, anxiety can sometimes cause dizziness or light-headedness.**

Changes in breathing and activation of the body's stress response may contribute to feelings of light-headedness or wooziness during anxiety. NIMH and Mayo Clinic both identify dizziness or light-headedness as possible symptoms associated with panic or anxiety.

Still, dizziness has many possible causes.

Talk with a healthcare professional if dizziness is:

- New or sudden
    
- Severe
    
- Recurrent
    
- Persistent
    
- Disrupting your daily activities
    
- Occurring without a clear explanation
    

Emergency evaluation is appropriate when severe dizziness occurs alongside symptoms such as chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, sudden weakness, confusion, or difficulty speaking.

---

## Can Anxiety Cause Nausea or Stomach Problems?

**Yes. Anxiety can affect the digestive system and may cause nausea or other stomach symptoms.**

When you are anxious, your body's priorities can shift toward responding to a perceived threat rather than routine digestion. As a result, some people notice nausea, stomach discomfort, indigestion, appetite changes, bloating, or changes in bowel habits.

This is why you might suddenly feel sick before an exam, interview, presentation, difficult conversation, or stressful event.

However, persistent nausea, abdominal pain, vomiting, bowel changes, or appetite changes should not automatically be attributed to anxiety. A healthcare professional can help determine whether another explanation needs to be considered.

---

## Can Anxiety Cause Headaches?

**Anxiety and prolonged stress can be associated with headaches.**

Muscle tension, particularly around the neck, shoulders, jaw, and scalp, may contribute to headache symptoms for some people. Anxiety and chronic stress can also affect sleep and other habits that influence how people feel physically. NIMH lists headaches and muscle aches among possible physical symptoms associated with generalized anxiety disorder.

Not every headache is caused by anxiety.

Seek medical advice for headaches that are severe, sudden, unusual for you, persistent, or accompanied by other concerning symptoms.

---

## Can Anxiety Cause Shortness of Breath?

**Yes, anxiety can affect breathing and create a sensation of breathlessness.**

During anxiety, breathing may become faster or feel different. Some people describe feeling as though they cannot get a satisfying breath, even though they are trying to breathe normally. Breathlessness is recognized as a possible symptom of anxiety and panic.

But there is an important distinction:

**Feeling breathless during anxiety does not prove that anxiety is causing the breathing problem.**

Asthma, heart conditions, infections, anemia, and other medical problems can also cause shortness of breath.

If you are having significant, new, sudden, or worsening difficulty breathing, seek medical attention rather than assuming it is anxiety.

## Why Do Anxiety Symptoms Feel So Real?

One of the most confusing parts of anxiety can be the physical sensations themselves.

You might think:

> "If this is anxiety, why does my body actually hurt?"

The answer is that anxiety involves real physiological changes.

When the body's alarm system becomes activated, your heart rate can change, breathing can change, muscles can tense, sweating can increase, and digestion can be affected. These are physical processes—not imaginary symptoms.

So if anxiety gives you a racing heart or tense muscles, saying **"it's all in your head"** is misleading.

At the same time, paying close attention to bodily sensations can make them feel even more noticeable. When a person becomes frightened by a physical sensation, that fear can increase anxiety, which may intensify the sensation.

This can create a feedback loop.

## Can Anxiety Cause Multiple Physical Symptoms at Once?

Yes.

Anxiety can affect several body systems simultaneously. During an intense episode, a person might experience:

- Racing or pounding heart
    
- Rapid breathing
    
- Sweating
    
- Trembling
    
- Dizziness
    
- Nausea
    
- Chest discomfort
    
- Muscle tension
    
- Tingling
    

Panic attacks, for example, can involve several of these symptoms at the same time.

Experiencing several symptoms together **does not confirm that anxiety is the cause**, though. Physical symptoms can overlap with many medical conditions, which is why concerning or unexplained symptoms deserve appropriate evaluation.

## Can Anxiety Cause Physical Symptoms Every Day?

**It can.** Persistent anxiety or prolonged stress may be associated with recurring physical symptoms.

Someone who feels anxious much of the time might repeatedly notice muscle tension, headaches, fatigue, stomach problems, sleep difficulties, sweating, or a racing heartbeat.

But daily physical symptoms should not automatically be labeled anxiety.

If symptoms occur every day, continue for a long time, are worsening, or interfere with work, school, sleep, relationships, or normal activities, consider talking with a healthcare professional.

An evaluation can help determine whether anxiety may be contributing, whether another health issue needs attention, or whether both are present.

---

## How Long Do Physical Symptoms of Anxiety Last?

There is no single amount of time that anxiety-related physical symptoms are supposed to last.

Some symptoms may settle as the stressful situation passes and the body's alarm response decreases. Others may continue when anxiety or stress remains elevated.

During a panic attack, physical symptoms often develop rapidly and may peak within minutes, although the overall experience can vary from person to person.

If physical symptoms persist, repeatedly return, worsen, or have no clear explanation, don't assume that anxiety is responsible. Appropriate medical evaluation may be important.

---

## Can Anxiety Symptoms Come and Go?

Yes. Anxiety-related physical symptoms can fluctuate.

They may become more noticeable during:

- Periods of increased stress
    
- Worry or fear
    
- Poor sleep
    
- Certain stressful situations
    
- High caffeine intake
    
- Periods of emotional strain
    

Symptoms may then decrease when the person feels safer or calmer.

However, a symptom coming and going does **not** prove that anxiety is causing it. Many physical conditions can also cause symptoms that fluctuate.

## How Can I Reduce Physical Symptoms of Anxiety?

If you believe anxiety may be contributing to physical discomfort, several simple strategies may help.

### 1. Try slow, steady breathing

Instead of taking repeated large breaths, aim for comfortable, gentle breathing. Let the breath become slower and steadier without forcing it or holding your breath for long periods.

If breathing exercises make you feel worse, stop and return to your normal breathing.

### 2. Relax tense muscles

Check your body for areas that may be tightening without you noticing.

Let your:

- Jaw soften
    
- Shoulders drop
    
- Hands unclench
    
- Forehead relax
    
- Neck loosen
    

Reducing unnecessary muscle tension can be a useful way to signal that you are not currently required to respond to a threat.

### 3. Ground yourself

Try bringing your attention back to the present moment.

For example, notice:

- Five things you can see
    
- Four things you can physically feel
    
- Three things you can hear
    
- Two things you can smell
    
- One thing you can taste
    

The goal is not to force anxiety away. It is to shift attention away from escalating worry and toward what is happening around you now.

### 4. Reduce excessive caffeine

Caffeine can intensify anxious sensations for some people, including feelings of jitteriness or a racing heart. If you notice a connection, consider reducing your intake and observing whether your symptoms change.

### 5. Get regular sleep

Consistent, adequate sleep supports both physical and emotional wellbeing. Poor sleep can make it harder to cope with stress and may leave you feeling more physically drained.

### 6. Move your body

Regular physical activity can be a useful part of managing stress and supporting mental wellbeing. Choose movement that is appropriate for your health and fitness level rather than treating exercise as a way to "burn off" anxiety.

### 7. Talk to someone you trust

Talking with a friend, family member, therapist, or another trusted person can make anxiety feel less isolating.

### 8. Identify patterns

Consider keeping a simple record of:

- When symptoms appear
    
- What you were doing beforehand
    
- What you were worried about
    
- How much you slept
    
- Caffeine intake
    
- Whether symptoms improved after the situation changed
    

Patterns can provide useful information to discuss with a healthcare professional.

These strategies may help reduce anxiety-related discomfort, but they are not guaranteed to eliminate symptoms or determine their cause.

## Can Anxiety Physical Symptoms Become a Cycle?

Yes. For some people, physical sensations and fear can reinforce each other.

A simplified cycle can look like this:

**Physical sensation → fear about the sensation → increased anxiety → stronger physical sensation → more fear**

For example, you might notice your heart beating faster.

You think:

**"Why is my heart racing? Something must be wrong."**

That thought increases your fear. Your body's stress response becomes stronger, and your heart may beat even faster. The stronger sensation then makes you more frightened.

This kind of feedback loop can occur during panic and intense anxiety.

Understanding the cycle can help you respond with less fear—for example, by slowing down, grounding yourself, and reminding yourself that anxiety can produce physical sensations.

But understanding the cycle should **never** mean ignoring potentially serious symptoms. If a symptom is new, severe, unusual, or concerning, seek appropriate medical care.

## Anxiety vs. a Physical Health Problem

Anxiety symptoms can overlap with symptoms of many physical conditions. There is no symptom checklist that can reliably tell you whether a particular sensation is caused by anxiety.

|Possible anxiety-related pattern|Reasons to consider medical evaluation|
|---|---|
|Symptoms appear during stress or fear|Symptoms are new or unexplained|
|Symptoms occur alongside other anxiety symptoms|Symptoms are severe or worsening|
|Symptoms improve as anxiety settles|Symptoms persist|
|Symptoms resemble previous anxiety episodes|Symptoms are different from previous episodes|
|Symptoms are linked to an identifiable anxiety trigger|Symptoms repeatedly occur without an obvious explanation|

**This comparison cannot determine the cause of physical symptoms.**

A healthcare professional may consider your symptoms, medical history, examination, and—when appropriate—tests to determine whether another health condition could be responsible. MedlinePlus notes that healthcare providers may perform a physical examination or laboratory tests when evaluating anxiety symptoms to rule out other medical causes.

It is also possible for anxiety and a physical health condition to occur together.

## When Should I See a Doctor?

Consider medical evaluation when:

- Symptoms are new
    
- Symptoms are severe
    
- Symptoms persist
    
- Symptoms are getting worse
    
- Symptoms repeatedly occur without an obvious trigger
    
- Symptoms interfere with daily activities
    
- You are unsure whether symptoms are anxiety-related
    
- You have an underlying health concern
    

You do not need to prove that your symptoms are caused by anxiety before seeking help.

A doctor or other qualified healthcare professional can help assess physical symptoms and determine what evaluation may be appropriate.

---

## When Should I Seek Emergency Medical Help?

Do not automatically assume a potentially serious physical symptom is anxiety.

Seek **local emergency medical care/services** for symptoms that could represent a medical emergency, such as:

- New or severe chest pain
    
- Sudden or severe unexplained chest discomfort
    
- Severe difficulty breathing
    
- Fainting or loss of consciousness
    
- Sudden severe weakness
    
- Sudden neurological changes such as difficulty speaking or weakness on one side
    
- Other severe or rapidly worsening symptoms
    

New or unexplained chest pain, particularly when accompanied by breathing difficulty, fainting, or other concerning symptoms, warrants urgent assessment.

If you are in immediate danger, experiencing suicidal thoughts, or thinking about harming yourself, seek urgent mental-health support or local emergency services immediately.

---

## When Should I Talk to a Mental-Health Professional?

Mental-health support may be useful when anxiety:

- Is persistent
    
- Is difficult to control
    
- Causes significant distress
    
- Interferes with sleep
    
- Affects work or school
    
- Affects relationships
    
- Causes avoidance
    
- Repeatedly produces distressing physical symptoms
    

A primary-care doctor, therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist can help assess what you are experiencing and discuss appropriate treatment or support.

You do not have to wait until anxiety becomes overwhelming before asking for help. NIMH notes that anxiety disorders can interfere with everyday activities and that treatment and support can help people manage symptoms.
