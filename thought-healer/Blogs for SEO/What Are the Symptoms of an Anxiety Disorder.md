# What Are the Symptoms of an Anxiety Disorder? Signs to Know

Feeling anxious from time to time is a normal part of life. You might feel nervous before an interview, worried about an important decision, or tense during a major life change. But anxiety disorders involve more than occasional worry. They can involve persistent or excessive fear or anxiety that is difficult to control and may affect thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, behavior, sleep, and everyday functioning.

**The symptoms of an anxiety disorder can include excessive worry, persistent fear, feeling on edge, racing thoughts, difficulty concentrating, sleep problems, avoidance, and physical symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating, dizziness, trembling, muscle tension, or stomach discomfort. However, having one or more of these symptoms does not necessarily mean you have an anxiety disorder.** A qualified healthcare or mental-health professional looks at the overall pattern, severity, duration, distress, impact on daily life, and possible other causes.

## What Are the Symptoms of an Anxiety Disorder?

Anxiety can affect people in different ways. Some people mainly experience persistent worry or fear, while others notice physical sensations such as a pounding heart or tense muscles. For some, anxiety shows up primarily through avoidance or repeated reassurance-seeking.

The symptoms can broadly be understood in four groups:

### Emotional symptoms

These may include:

- Persistent fear or apprehension
    
- Excessive worry
    
- Feeling constantly “on edge”
    
- Irritability
    
- Feeling overwhelmed
    
- Difficulty relaxing
    
- A sense that something bad may happen
    

### Cognitive symptoms

These may include:

- Racing thoughts
    
- Difficulty controlling worry
    
- Repeatedly imagining worst-case scenarios
    
- Expecting negative outcomes
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Trouble tolerating uncertainty
    
- Persistent thoughts about possible problems
    

### Physical symptoms

Anxiety may also cause physical sensations such as:

- Rapid or pounding heartbeat
    
- Sweating
    
- Trembling or shaking
    
- Muscle tension
    
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
    
- Headaches
    
- Stomach discomfort or nausea
    
- Rapid breathing or shortness of breath
    
- Fatigue
    
- Sleep difficulties
    

These symptoms are commonly described in anxiety disorders, but they can also occur for many other physical or psychological reasons.

### Behavioral symptoms

Anxiety can influence what a person does—or avoids doing. Behavioral signs may include:

- Avoiding places or situations that cause fear
    
- Cancelling plans because of anxiety
    
- Avoiding difficult conversations
    
- Procrastinating because of fear of making a mistake
    
- Repeatedly asking other people for reassurance
    
- Checking things repeatedly
    
- Constantly researching possible problems
    
- Withdrawing from activities that were previously enjoyable
    

Avoidance can provide short-term relief, but in some anxiety conditions it can also reinforce fear and make situations feel increasingly difficult over time.

## Common Physical Symptoms of Anxiety

Physical symptoms are one reason people may wonder whether anxiety is affecting them.

When the body's stress response is activated, it can produce real physical sensations. You might notice your heart beating faster, your muscles becoming tense, your breathing changing, or your stomach feeling unsettled.

### Racing or pounding heart

You may notice your heart beating faster or harder than usual. During intense anxiety or panic, heart palpitations can feel particularly noticeable.

### Rapid breathing or shortness of breath

Anxiety can change your breathing pattern. Some people feel as though they cannot get a satisfying breath or find themselves breathing faster than usual.

### Sweating and trembling

Feeling hot, sweaty, shaky, or physically restless can occur when the body responds to perceived danger or stress.

### Muscle tension

You may unconsciously tighten your jaw, shoulders, neck, or other muscles. Persistent tension can sometimes contribute to aches or headaches.

### Dizziness or lightheadedness

Some people experience dizziness or lightheadedness during periods of intense anxiety. These sensations can be frightening, particularly when they are unexpected.

### Stomach problems

Anxiety can be associated with nausea, stomach discomfort, digestive changes, or the feeling of having “butterflies” in the stomach.

### Fatigue and sleep problems

An anxious mind may make it difficult to relax at bedtime. Ongoing anxiety can also leave someone feeling mentally and physically tired.

### Tingling or numbness

During intense anxiety or panic, some people experience tingling or numb sensations, particularly in the hands or other parts of the body. Panic attacks can also involve chest pain, dizziness, difficulty breathing, sweating, trembling, and nausea.

**Important:** physical symptoms should not automatically be assumed to be caused by anxiety. Heart, thyroid, respiratory, medication-related, substance-related, and other health conditions can produce symptoms that resemble anxiety. If symptoms are new, severe, unusual, persistent, or concerning, appropriate medical evaluation is important.

## Common Emotional and Mental Symptoms

Anxiety is not simply “being nervous.” For someone experiencing significant anxiety, worry can become difficult to switch off.

You might find yourself repeatedly thinking:

> “What if something goes wrong?”

> “What if I can't handle it?”

> “What if something happens to someone I care about?”

Occasional concerns like these are normal. The concern becomes greater when worry is persistent, excessive, difficult to control, or begins interfering with everyday life.

Common mental and emotional symptoms can include:

- Excessive worry about everyday situations
    
- Persistent fear
    
- Feeling constantly alert or on edge
    
- Difficulty relaxing
    
- Racing thoughts
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Irritability
    
- Fear of losing control
    
- Repeatedly anticipating negative outcomes
    
- Difficulty dealing with uncertainty
    

For example, someone might finish one task at work and immediately begin worrying about the next possible problem. Another person may spend hours replaying a conversation and wondering whether they said something wrong.

These experiences do not automatically indicate an anxiety disorder. What matters is the broader pattern and how significantly it affects the person's life.

## Behavioral Signs of Anxiety

Anxiety does not always look like visible nervousness. Sometimes it appears as changes in behavior.

Someone may begin avoiding a particular place, postpone an important task, cancel social plans, or repeatedly ask another person, “Are you sure everything is okay?”

Other behavioral patterns can include:

- Avoiding situations that trigger anxiety
    
- Checking locks, messages, symptoms, or other things repeatedly
    
- Seeking frequent reassurance
    
- Avoiding phone calls or conversations
    
- Delaying decisions because of fear
    
- Researching possible problems repeatedly
    
- Withdrawing from hobbies or social activities
    

Avoidance can make sense emotionally: avoiding something frightening may bring immediate relief. However, repeatedly avoiding feared situations can sometimes prevent a person from learning that they can cope with them. This is one reason anxiety can become self-reinforcing for some people.

## What Does an Anxiety Disorder Feel Like?

There is no single way that anxiety feels.

For one person, it may feel like a mind that never seems to switch off. For another, it may feel mainly physical—a racing heart, tense muscles, stomach discomfort, or difficulty breathing.

A typical day might look something like this:

You wake up already thinking about everything that could go wrong. During work or school, you struggle to focus because your thoughts keep returning to possible problems. You feel tense and find it difficult to relax. Later, you avoid something because you are worried about what might happen. At night, your body is tired, but your mind continues going over the day's concerns.

Not everyone with anxiety experiences this pattern, and symptoms can vary considerably between people and over time.

## How Is Anxiety Different From Normal Stress?

Stress and anxiety overlap, but they are not identical.

Stress is generally a response to an external pressure or situation. Anxiety can occur as a response to stress but can also continue when there is no immediate threat. Occasional anxiety is a normal human experience. Anxiety disorders involve symptoms that can become persistent, excessive, difficult to control, or disruptive to daily life.

|Normal or occasional anxiety|Anxiety that may warrant evaluation|
|---|---|
|Often connected to a specific situation|May feel persistent or excessive|
|Usually temporary|May continue or repeatedly return|
|Usually manageable|May feel difficult to control|
|Often settles when the situation passes|May continue despite little immediate danger|
|Limited impact on daily life|May interfere with work, school, relationships, sleep, or activities|

This comparison is for education—not a diagnostic test.

## How Long Do Anxiety Symptoms Last?

There is no single duration that applies to every anxiety experience.

Temporary anxiety may last for a short period around a stressful event. Other symptoms may recur periodically, while some anxiety disorders involve longer-lasting patterns.

Panic attacks, for example, can involve sudden episodes of intense fear and physical symptoms. The episode itself may resolve with time, while fear about having another attack can continue afterward.

For generalized anxiety disorder, persistent and difficult-to-control worry is an important part of the clinical picture. NIMH notes that GAD involves excessive worry occurring more days than not for at least six months, alongside other symptoms and meaningful impairment.

This does **not** mean that you should use a specific timeframe as a self-diagnosis test. Different anxiety disorders have different diagnostic patterns.

## Different Anxiety Disorders and Their Symptoms

“Anxiety disorder” is an umbrella term for several conditions. Their symptoms overlap, but the central fear or worry can differ.

### Generalized anxiety disorder

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) involves excessive worry across multiple areas of life, such as work, finances, health, family, or everyday responsibilities. People may find it difficult to control their worry and may experience restlessness, irritability, concentration problems, muscle tension, fatigue, or sleep difficulties.

### Panic disorder

Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks along with ongoing concern about additional attacks or changes in behavior related to them.

A panic attack can involve intense fear and physical symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating, trembling, dizziness, difficulty breathing, chest pain, or nausea.

Having a panic attack does not automatically mean someone has panic disorder.

### Social anxiety disorder

Social anxiety disorder involves significant fear or anxiety in situations where a person may be judged, scrutinized, embarrassed, or rejected by others.

Examples can include public speaking, meeting unfamiliar people, interviews, eating in front of others, or participating in social situations. People may avoid these situations or experience intense discomfort when facing them.

### Specific phobias

Specific phobias involve intense fear related to a particular object or situation, such as certain animals, heights, flying, or other specific triggers.

The fear may be much stronger than the actual danger associated with the situation and can lead to avoidance.

### Separation anxiety disorder

Separation anxiety disorder involves excessive fear or distress related to separation from an attachment figure. It can occur in children and adults and is more than ordinary concern about being apart from someone important.

A professional assessment is required to determine whether symptoms fit a particular anxiety disorder.

## Anxiety Symptoms in Different Situations

Anxiety can appear differently depending on what triggers or maintains it.

**At work:** You may worry excessively about making mistakes, meeting expectations, speaking in meetings, or being evaluated.

**At school:** Anxiety may involve fear of exams, presentations, academic performance, social situations, or making mistakes.

**Socially:** You may worry about being judged, embarrassed, rejected, or negatively evaluated.

**Health-related:** You may repeatedly worry about physical sensations or what they might mean.

**At night:** Worries may become more noticeable when distractions disappear and you are trying to sleep.

**During major life changes:** Moving, starting a new job, relationship changes, financial uncertainty, illness, or other major events can increase anxiety for some people.

The context can provide useful information, but it does not by itself establish a diagnosis.

## Can Anxiety Cause Physical Symptoms?

**Yes. Anxiety can produce genuine physical symptoms.** The body's stress response can affect heart rate, breathing, muscles, digestion, sweating, and other physical processes.

But physical symptoms are **not proof that anxiety is the cause**.

For example, a racing heart, dizziness, chest discomfort, or shortness of breath can have many possible explanations. A healthcare professional may consider medical history, medications, substances, and physical health conditions when evaluating unexplained symptoms.

If a physical symptom is new, severe, unusual, persistent, or worrying you, it is reasonable to have it evaluated rather than assuming it is anxiety.

## Can Anxiety Symptoms Come and Go?

Yes. Anxiety symptoms can fluctuate.

Some people notice stronger symptoms during periods of:

- Increased stress
    
- Poor sleep
    
- Major life changes
    
- Uncertainty
    
- Ongoing conflict
    
- Illness or other stressful circumstances
    

Caffeine and other substances can also worsen anxiety symptoms for some people.

Symptoms coming and going does not prove that anxiety is—or is not—the underlying cause. A professional considers the broader pattern.

## What Can Make Anxiety Symptoms Worse?

Several factors may contribute to increased anxiety for some people, including:

- Chronic stress
    
- Lack of sleep
    
- Excessive caffeine
    
- Alcohol or substance use
    
- Major life changes
    
- Ongoing conflict
    
- Lack of routine
    
- Avoidance of feared situations
    

These factors are not necessarily the cause of an anxiety disorder. Anxiety disorders can involve a combination of biological, psychological, genetic, and environmental influences.

## What Should I Do If I Recognize These Symptoms?

Recognizing anxiety symptoms can be unsettling, but you do not need to figure everything out by yourself.

### 1. Track your symptoms

For a few days, consider noting:

- What symptoms you experience
    
- When they happen
    
- What was happening beforehand
    
- Possible triggers
    
- How well you slept
    
- Caffeine or other substances you used
    
- Major stressful events
    
- How symptoms affect your daily activities
    

A simple record can help you and a healthcare professional identify patterns.

### 2. Talk to someone you trust

Talking with a trusted friend, family member, or other supportive person can reduce the feeling that you have to manage everything alone.

### 3. Consider professional evaluation

A doctor, psychologist, therapist, psychiatrist, or other qualified professional can help determine what may be contributing to your symptoms.

### 4. Support your overall wellbeing

Helpful habits can include:

- Maintaining a consistent sleep routine
    
- Staying physically active
    
- Eating regular, balanced meals
    
- Staying connected with supportive people
    
- Practicing relaxation techniques
    
- Reducing excessive caffeine
    

These habits may support wellbeing and symptom management, but they are not guaranteed treatments for an anxiety disorder.

## How Are Anxiety Disorders Diagnosed?

An anxiety disorder is not diagnosed simply by counting symptoms.

A healthcare or mental-health professional may consider:

- The type of symptoms
    
- How frequently they occur
    
- How long they have been present
    
- Their severity
    
- How difficult they are to control
    
- The amount of distress they cause
    
- Their impact on work, school, relationships, sleep, and other activities
    
- Medical history
    
- Medications and substance use
    
- Other mental-health symptoms
    
- Possible physical causes
    

A healthcare provider may ask when symptoms began, how often they occur, and how they affect your life. Depending on the situation, medical evaluation may also be used to rule out other possible explanations.

In other words, **symptoms provide information; the overall clinical picture guides diagnosis.**

## Can an Online Anxiety Test Diagnose Me?

**No. An online questionnaire cannot independently diagnose an anxiety disorder.**

Some validated questionnaires can be useful as **screening tools**. Screening can indicate that further assessment may be worthwhile, but it is different from receiving a diagnosis.

Think of it this way:

**Screening = “Should I consider getting assessed?”**

**Diagnosis = “Does a qualified professional determine that I meet the criteria for a specific condition?”**

Be cautious about random online quizzes that claim to tell you definitively whether you have an anxiety disorder.

## When Should I See a Mental-Health Professional?

Consider seeking professional support if anxiety symptoms:

- Persist or keep returning
    
- Become difficult to control
    
- Cause significant distress
    
- Interfere with sleep
    
- Affect work or school
    
- Affect relationships
    
- Lead you to avoid important situations
    
- Interfere with everyday activities
    
- Are becoming more frequent or severe
    

You do not need to wait until anxiety becomes overwhelming before asking for help.

Seeking an evaluation does not mean that you definitely have an anxiety disorder. It simply gives you an opportunity to understand what is happening and discuss appropriate support.

## When Are Anxiety-Like Symptoms an Emergency?

Some symptoms associated with anxiety can overlap with medical emergencies.

If you experience **new, severe, unusual, or concerning symptoms**—such as severe chest pain, severe difficulty breathing, fainting, or another alarming physical symptom—seek urgent medical attention rather than automatically assuming the symptoms are anxiety.

If you are in immediate danger, or are experiencing suicidal thoughts or thoughts of harming yourself, seek emergency help immediately or contact an appropriate crisis service.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. What are the most common symptoms of an anxiety disorder?

Common symptoms can include excessive worry or fear, feeling restless or on edge, difficulty relaxing, trouble concentrating, sleep problems, avoidance, and physical symptoms such as sweating, trembling, a racing heart, dizziness, muscle tension, or stomach discomfort.

### 2. What are the physical symptoms of anxiety?

Physical symptoms may include a rapid or pounding heartbeat, sweating, trembling, muscle tension, dizziness, rapid breathing, shortness of breath, fatigue, headaches, nausea, stomach discomfort, and sleep difficulties.

### 3. What does anxiety feel like?

Anxiety can feel like persistent worry, fear, tension, restlessness, racing thoughts, difficulty concentrating, or an inability to relax. Some people also experience noticeable physical sensations such as a racing heart, sweating, trembling, dizziness, or stomach discomfort.

### 4. How do I know if I have an anxiety disorder?

You cannot determine this from a symptom list alone. A qualified professional considers your symptoms, their duration and frequency, how difficult they are to control, their impact on your life, and other possible medical or psychological explanations.

### 5. Can anxiety cause chest pain?

Anxiety and panic can be associated with chest pain or discomfort. However, chest pain can also have other causes. New, severe, unusual, or concerning chest pain should not automatically be assumed to be anxiety and may require urgent medical evaluation.

### 6. Can anxiety cause dizziness?

Yes, dizziness or lightheadedness can occur during anxiety or panic. However, dizziness has many possible causes, so recurring or concerning dizziness should be discussed with a healthcare professional rather than automatically attributed to anxiety.

### 7. Can anxiety cause stomach problems?

Yes. Anxiety can be associated with nausea, stomach discomfort, digestive problems, and other gastrointestinal symptoms. Persistent digestive symptoms can have many causes, so they should not automatically be attributed to anxiety.

### 8. How long do anxiety symptoms last?

There is no universal duration. Some anxiety is temporary and linked to a stressful event, while symptoms associated with anxiety disorders can persist or recur. Different anxiety disorders also have different clinical patterns.

### 9. Can anxiety symptoms come and go?

Yes. Symptoms can fluctuate and may become more noticeable during periods of stress, poor sleep, major changes, or uncertainty. Fluctuating symptoms alone do not confirm or rule out an anxiety disorder.

### 10. When should I seek help for anxiety?

Consider professional support when anxiety persists, becomes difficult to control, causes significant distress, affects sleep or relationships, interferes with work or school, leads to avoidance, or is becoming more frequent or severe.

## The Bottom Line

Anxiety can affect much more than your thoughts. It can influence your emotions, concentration, physical sensations, behavior, sleep, relationships, and ability to manage everyday activities.

The most important point is that **experiencing anxiety symptoms does not automatically mean you have an anxiety disorder**. Occasional anxiety is a normal part of life, while an anxiety disorder involves a broader pattern that may be persistent, excessive, difficult to control, distressing, or disruptive.

If you are concerned about symptoms, you do not have to diagnose yourself. Keeping track of what you experience and speaking with a qualified healthcare or mental-health professional can be a useful next step.

Anxiety disorders are treatable, and seeking help early can make it easier to understand what you are experiencing and find appropriate support.
