# ADHD or Anxiety? How Do You Know Which One You Have?

If you are struggling with concentration, forgetfulness, procrastination, restlessness, racing thoughts, poor organization, or difficulty finishing tasks, it is understandable to wonder: **“Do I have ADHD or anxiety?”**

The difficulty is that ADHD and anxiety can look remarkably similar from the outside. Both can affect concentration, memory, sleep, productivity, task completion, and emotional well-being.

But **symptoms alone cannot reliably tell you whether you have ADHD, anxiety, both, or something else**.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition involving persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that begin during childhood. Anxiety disorders involve excessive or persistent fear, worry, apprehension, or related physical and behavioral symptoms. A professional assessment looks at the pattern, timing, developmental history, severity, functional impact, and possible alternative explanations.

So if you recognize yourself in descriptions of both conditions, that does not mean you have to choose one. **ADHD and anxiety can occur together.**

# What Is ADHD?

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD, is a **neurodevelopmental disorder** characterized by persistent difficulties involving inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, or a combination of these features. ADHD symptoms begin during childhood, although they may not be recognized until adolescence or adulthood.

In adults, ADHD may involve:

- difficulty sustaining attention
    
- becoming distracted easily
    
- disorganization
    
- procrastination
    
- trouble starting or completing tasks
    
- difficulty managing time
    
- frequently losing things
    
- forgetfulness
    
- difficulty following through on plans
    
- fidgeting or internal restlessness
    
- impulsive decisions or interruptions
    

Adult ADHD does not always look like the stereotypical image of a hyperactive child. Hyperactivity may become less obvious with age and instead feel like **internal restlessness, difficulty relaxing, or a constant need to stay occupied**.

It is also important to understand that ADHD is not simply being distracted, disorganized, or “bad at time management.” Many people experience these difficulties occasionally. ADHD involves a persistent pattern that causes meaningful impairment and occurs across relevant areas of life.

# What Is Anxiety?

Anxiety is a normal human response to perceived danger, uncertainty, pressure, or important life events. Feeling nervous before an interview or worrying about an upcoming deadline does not automatically mean you have an anxiety disorder.

Anxiety disorders are different. They involve anxiety, fear, or worry that is excessive, persistent, difficult to control, or disruptive to everyday life. There are several types, including generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, and phobia-related disorders.

Anxiety can affect several parts of the body and mind.

### Cognitive symptoms may include:

- excessive worrying
    
- difficulty concentrating
    
- repetitive “what if?” thoughts
    
- anticipating negative outcomes
    
- difficulty making decisions
    
- mentally reviewing possible problems
    

### Physical symptoms may include:

- feeling restless or on edge
    
- muscle tension
    
- sweating
    
- trembling
    
- shortness of breath
    
- fatigue
    
- difficulty sleeping
    
- headaches or other tension-related symptoms
    

For example, generalized anxiety disorder can involve persistent, difficult-to-control worry alongside symptoms such as restlessness, concentration difficulties, irritability, muscle tension, fatigue, and sleep problems.

# Why ADHD and Anxiety Can Look So Similar

The **ADHD-anxiety overlap** is one reason self-diagnosis can be difficult.

Both conditions may be associated with:

- difficulty concentrating
    
- forgetfulness
    
- procrastination
    
- restlessness
    
- sleep difficulties
    
- irritability
    
- feeling overwhelmed
    
- difficulty completing tasks
    
- poor productivity
    
- difficulty making decisions
    

The more useful question is often not simply **“Do I have this symptom?”** but **“What seems to be happening when this symptom occurs?”**

Imagine two people sitting down to complete an important report.

One person opens the document, gets distracted by messages, switches between browser tabs, remembers another task, starts that task, and eventually forgets what they originally intended to do.

Another person opens the same document but becomes preoccupied with thoughts such as, “What if I make a mistake? What if my manager doesn't like it? What if I can't finish it on time?” Their attention repeatedly returns to those worries.

Both people may say:

> “I can't concentrate.”

But the experiences underneath that statement may be quite different.

That distinction is useful during a professional assessment, but it is **not a diagnostic test by itself**.

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Differences in Thought Patterns

Thought patterns can sometimes provide useful clues to discuss with a clinician.

## ADHD may involve:

- rapidly shifting thoughts
    
- jumping between unrelated ideas
    
- becoming distracted by external stimuli
    
- losing track of what you were doing
    
- difficulty holding information in mind
    
- difficulty organizing several thoughts or tasks
    

Someone with ADHD might think:

> “I need to answer that email. Oh, I should also book that appointment. Wait, I forgot to finish the report. Let me check something quickly…”

The issue may be that attention keeps moving from one thing to another.

## Anxiety may involve:

- repetitive worry
    
- anticipating negative outcomes
    
- fear-based thinking
    
- “what if?” thoughts
    
- mentally reviewing possible problems
    
- difficulty switching away from worries
    

Someone experiencing anxiety might think:

> “What if I misunderstood the instructions? What if I make a mistake? What if they think I'm not capable?”

The thoughts may stay focused on perceived threats, uncertainty, or negative outcomes.

However, **people can experience both patterns**. ADHD racing thoughts and anxiety racing thoughts are not always cleanly separable.

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Concentration Problems

### Why can't I concentrate—is it ADHD or anxiety?

Both ADHD and anxiety can interfere with concentration.

With ADHD, concentration problems may involve difficulty sustaining attention, resisting distraction, organizing tasks, or completing lengthy activities. Adults with ADHD can particularly struggle when a task is repetitive, uninteresting, lengthy, or poorly structured.

With anxiety, concentration can be disrupted because attention is repeatedly pulled toward worries, uncertainty, perceived threats, or physical sensations.

Poor sleep, chronic stress, burnout, depression, medications, and other health issues can also affect concentration.

Therefore, **concentration problems alone cannot distinguish ADHD from anxiety**.

---

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Restlessness

Restlessness can occur in ADHD, anxiety, ordinary stress, and several other circumstances.

ADHD-related restlessness may involve:

- fidgeting
    
- difficulty remaining still
    
- feeling internally restless
    
- needing frequent activity or stimulation
    

Anxiety-related restlessness may occur alongside:

- feeling constantly on edge
    
- muscle tension
    
- heightened physical arousal
    
- difficulty relaxing
    
- sweating or trembling
    
- rapid heartbeat or shortness of breath
    

NIMH lists restlessness and difficulty relaxing among symptoms that can occur with generalized anxiety disorder, while adult ADHD can include fidgeting and internal restlessness.

But no single physical sensation proves that someone has either condition.

---

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Procrastination and Avoidance

Procrastination can have different underlying reasons.

### ADHD-related difficulties may include:

- difficulty getting started
    
- not knowing how to break a task into steps
    
- poor time estimation
    
- becoming distracted
    
- difficulty sustaining effort
    
- finding repetitive tasks especially difficult to initiate
    

### Anxiety-related difficulties may include:

- fear of failure
    
- perfectionism
    
- fear of criticism or judgment
    
- worry about making the wrong decision
    
- avoiding uncertainty
    
- feeling overwhelmed by anticipated consequences
    

For example, someone might delay sending an email because they keep forgetting to return to it. Another person might delay sending it because they have rewritten it repeatedly out of fear that it is not good enough.

Both can look like **“procrastination.”**

The reason behind the behavior can therefore be more informative than the behavior itself.

---

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Forgetfulness

Forgetfulness can also happen for multiple reasons.

With ADHD, someone may forget information because their attention was divided or because they did not fully engage with or organize the information when it was presented. Adults with ADHD may also frequently lose things or forget everyday tasks.

Anxiety can also interfere with concentration and memory when persistent worry consumes mental attention.

For example, someone may walk into a room and forget why they went there because they were distracted by several competing thoughts. Someone experiencing significant anxiety may forget information because their attention was heavily occupied by worrying.

Sleep deprivation and chronic stress can make either situation worse.

---

# ADHD vs Anxiety: Time Management

Time-management problems can appear in both ADHD and anxiety, but potentially for different reasons.

### With ADHD, difficulties may involve:

- underestimating how long tasks will take
    
- losing track of time
    
- difficulty prioritizing
    
- difficulty transitioning between tasks
    
- delaying tasks until a deadline creates urgency
    

### With anxiety, difficulties may involve:

- excessive checking
    
- perfectionism
    
- repeatedly reconsidering decisions
    
- avoidance
    
- spending large amounts of time worrying
    
- difficulty acting because of uncertainty
    

Either pattern can eventually result in missed deadlines, unfinished tasks, and overwhelm.

# Can You Have ADHD and Anxiety at the Same Time?

**Yes. A person can have both ADHD and an anxiety disorder.**

Having anxiety does not automatically rule out ADHD, and having ADHD does not mean that every experience of anxiety is caused by ADHD.

ADHD frequently occurs alongside other mental-health conditions, including anxiety, and overlapping symptoms can make assessment more complicated.

This is important because focusing on only one condition may leave part of the person's experience unexplained.

For example, someone could have longstanding ADHD-related difficulties with organization and attention while also experiencing anxiety that developed later around work, relationships, health, or other concerns.

Understanding the full picture helps a professional determine what support may be appropriate.

# How Timing and Developmental History Can Help

One of the most important differences considered during an ADHD assessment is **when the symptoms began**.

ADHD is a developmental condition. Symptoms must have roots in childhood, even if nobody recognized them as ADHD at the time. Adults may not receive a diagnosis until much later because symptoms were subtle, circumstances provided enough structure, or increasing adult responsibilities eventually made longstanding difficulties more noticeable.

A clinician may therefore ask about:

- school experiences
    
- childhood organization
    
- forgotten homework or belongings
    
- longstanding difficulties following through
    
- attention problems across different settings
    
- early behavioral patterns
    

Anxiety can develop at different stages of life. Some people experience anxiety in childhood, while others develop significant symptoms later.

A clinician may therefore also explore:

- when worry first became problematic
    
- whether symptoms occur mainly during stressful periods
    
- whether concentration difficulties existed before anxiety became prominent
    
- whether attention problems continue even when the person feels relatively calm
    
- what situations trigger or worsen symptoms
    

Developmental history can provide important information, but remembering childhood experiences is not enough to diagnose yourself with ADHD.

---

# Could It Be Something Else?

Sometimes the answer is neither ADHD nor an anxiety disorder—or it may involve more than one factor.

Problems with concentration, memory, motivation, restlessness, or sleep can also be associated with:

- sleep deprivation
    
- chronic stress
    
- burnout
    
- depression
    
- trauma-related symptoms
    
- substance use
    
- medication effects
    
- physical health conditions
    
- other mental-health conditions
    

NIMH specifically notes that stress, sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, and physical conditions can produce symptoms that resemble ADHD.

That is one reason a proper evaluation matters.

# 10 Questions to Ask Yourself

These questions are **not an ADHD or anxiety test**. They can simply help you organize what you have been experiencing.

1. **Have these difficulties been present for many years?**
    
2. **Were similar problems noticeable during childhood?**
    
3. **Do concentration problems happen even when I am not worried?**
    
4. **When my worries settle down, do my attention problems remain?**
    
5. **Are my thoughts mainly rapidly shifting, or mainly focused on worry and possible threats?**
    
6. **Do I avoid tasks because I am afraid of the outcome, or because I cannot seem to get started?**
    
7. **Do I frequently lose track of time?**
    
8. **Do these difficulties affect work, education, relationships, or home responsibilities?**
    
9. **Have sleep problems or increased stress recently made everything worse?**
    
10. **Could another health or mental-health issue be contributing?**
    

These questions cannot diagnose ADHD or anxiety. **They are intended to help you describe your experiences more clearly when speaking with a professional.**

# ADHD and Anxiety Screening Tests

Online questionnaires and clinical screening tools can be useful starting points, but **screening is not the same as diagnosis**.

An ADHD screening questionnaire may identify symptoms that warrant further assessment. An anxiety questionnaire may similarly indicate that anxiety symptoms deserve closer attention.

However, overlapping symptoms can affect scores.

For example, difficulty concentrating may increase because of ADHD, anxiety, sleep problems, depression, or another issue. A screening score therefore needs to be interpreted in context.

A clinician may combine:

- standardized symptom questionnaires
    
- clinical interviews
    
- developmental history
    
- functional information
    
- medical and mental-health history
    
- information from other people when appropriate
    

NIMH notes that adult ADHD evaluations can include clinical interviews, behavior rating scales, symptom checklists, and assessment of medical and mental-health history.

If you use an online ADHD or anxiety screening tool, think of the result as a **conversation starter rather than a diagnosis**.

# How Professionals Distinguish ADHD From Anxiety

There is no single blood test, brain scan, or laboratory test that simply tells someone, “This is ADHD” or “This is anxiety.”

A qualified clinician usually looks at the **overall pattern**.

An assessment may explore:

- what symptoms you experience
    
- when they began
    
- how long they have lasted
    
- how frequently they occur
    
- whether they occur in multiple settings
    
- childhood history
    
- school or work functioning
    
- relationships
    
- emotional symptoms
    
- specific triggers
    
- sleep
    
- medical history
    
- medications
    
- substance use
    
- other mental-health symptoms
    
- how significantly the difficulties affect everyday functioning
    

For adult ADHD, developmental history is especially important because symptoms must have begun during childhood.

For anxiety, a clinician may explore the nature of worry or fear, how difficult it is to control, associated physical symptoms, duration, triggers, avoidance, and functional impact.

The goal is not simply to match you with a label. It is to understand **what is happening and what kind of help could be appropriate**.

# When Should I Seek Professional Help?

Consider speaking with a healthcare or mental-health professional if your difficulties:

- persist over time
    
- cause significant distress
    
- interfere with work or education
    
- affect relationships
    
- interfere with everyday responsibilities
    
- result in repeated missed obligations
    
- substantially disrupt sleep
    
- remain difficult to manage despite your efforts
    

You do not have to be certain that you have ADHD or anxiety before asking for help.

You can simply say:

> “I've been having persistent problems with concentration, organization, worry, and completing tasks, and I'm not sure what's causing them.”

That is enough to begin a conversation.

Seeking an evaluation does **not** mean that you necessarily have ADHD or an anxiety disorder.

---

# What to Do Before an Appointment

A little preparation can make it easier to explain what you have been experiencing.

Consider:

- writing down your main symptoms
    
- recording real examples from work, school, relationships, or home
    
- noting when the difficulties became noticeable
    
- thinking about whether similar patterns existed during childhood
    
- noting recent changes in stress or sleep
    
- making a list of current medications and relevant substances
    
- writing down questions you want to ask
    
- asking someone who knew you during childhood about longstanding patterns if that feels appropriate and comfortable
    

You do not need to produce a perfect history. Honest examples are usually more useful than trying to decide in advance whether something “counts” as ADHD or anxiety.

---

# What Can Help While You Figure It Out?

Some low-risk strategies can make daily life easier regardless of whether the underlying issue turns out to be ADHD, anxiety, both, or something else.

### Try practical supports such as:

- maintaining a consistent sleep schedule
    
- using calendars, reminders, and alarms
    
- breaking large tasks into smaller steps
    
- reducing unnecessary distractions
    
- writing down worries rather than repeatedly holding them in your head
    
- using grounding techniques when you feel anxious
    
- scheduling tasks realistically
    
- taking regular movement breaks
    
- limiting caffeine if you notice that it worsens anxiety or sleep
    
- asking trusted people for practical support
    

These strategies are not substitutes for professional assessment when symptoms are persistent or significantly impairing. They are simply tools that may help you function while you work out what is happening.

# Common Myths About ADHD and Anxiety

### “If I have anxiety, I can't have ADHD.”

Not true. ADHD and anxiety can occur together.

### “If I have ADHD, all my anxiety must be caused by ADHD.”

Not necessarily. Anxiety can have its own causes and may require separate assessment.

### “Being distracted means I have ADHD.”

Everyone becomes distracted sometimes. ADHD involves a persistent pattern of symptoms that causes impairment and has developmental roots.

### “Worrying a lot automatically means I have an anxiety disorder.”

Not necessarily. Anxiety is a normal emotion. Anxiety disorders involve more persistent, excessive, difficult-to-control, or impairing symptoms.

### “You can tell the difference from one symptom.”

Usually not. Concentration problems, restlessness, forgetfulness, and sleep difficulties can occur for many reasons.

### “An online quiz can diagnose me.”

No. Screening tools can identify symptoms worth discussing but do not replace a professional assessment.

### “ADHD and anxiety cannot happen together.”

They can. One condition does not exclude the other.

### “If I can focus sometimes, I can't have ADHD.”

Being able to focus in some situations does not by itself rule out ADHD. What matters is the broader pattern, persistence, context, and impact on functioning.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. How do I know if I have ADHD or anxiety?

You generally cannot determine this reliably from symptoms alone. ADHD and anxiety can both cause concentration problems, restlessness, procrastination, forgetfulness, and sleep difficulties. A professional assessment considers the symptoms' pattern, timing, developmental history, triggers, functional impact, and alternative explanations. Screening questionnaires can be useful but do not establish a diagnosis.

## 2. Can anxiety look like ADHD?

Yes. Anxiety can sometimes resemble ADHD because persistent worry can interfere with concentration, memory, task completion, sleep, and organization. Someone who is mentally occupied by worry may appear distracted or forgetful. The underlying pattern, timing, and broader history are important when determining what may be contributing to these difficulties.

## 3. Can ADHD cause anxiety?

ADHD and anxiety can be related, but it is not accurate to assume that ADHD automatically causes anxiety. A person with ADHD may experience frustration, overwhelm, repeated difficulties, or stressful situations that affect their emotional well-being. However, anxiety can also develop independently. A professional can assess whether both conditions may be present.

## 4. Can you have ADHD and anxiety at the same time?

Yes. ADHD and anxiety can occur together. When they overlap, it may be harder to determine which symptoms are related to which condition. For example, both can contribute to concentration difficulties or restlessness. An assessment that considers the full history can help identify the different factors involved.

## 5. What is the difference between ADHD and anxiety?

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition involving persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with developmental roots. Anxiety disorders primarily involve excessive or persistent fear, worry, apprehension, or related distress. The two can share symptoms such as poor concentration, restlessness, procrastination, and sleep problems.

## 6. Can anxiety cause concentration problems?

Yes. Anxiety can make concentration difficult when worry or threat-focused thinking repeatedly captures attention. Physical arousal and poor sleep can also interfere with functioning. However, concentration problems are not specific to anxiety and can occur with ADHD, depression, stress, sleep deprivation, medications, and other conditions.

## 7. Can ADHD cause racing thoughts?

Some people with ADHD describe rapidly shifting thoughts, frequent changes in attention, or difficulty maintaining one train of thought. However, “racing thoughts” is not specific to ADHD. Anxiety can produce repetitive worry and fast-moving thoughts as well. The content, pattern, context, and overall history matter more than the phrase itself.

## 8. Is procrastination a sign of ADHD or anxiety?

It can occur with either. ADHD may involve difficulty initiating, organizing, prioritizing, or sustaining tasks. Anxiety may lead to avoidance because of fear of failure, perfectionism, uncertainty, or judgment. Procrastination alone cannot distinguish between ADHD and anxiety, and it can have many other causes.

## 9. How is ADHD diagnosed in adults?

Adult ADHD diagnosis involves a clinical assessment rather than a single test. A provider may examine current symptoms, childhood history, functioning across settings, medical and mental-health history, and possible alternative explanations. Standardized rating scales or symptom checklists may be used as part of the evaluation.

## 10. Who should I talk to if I think I have ADHD or anxiety?

You can start with a primary-care clinician or a qualified mental-health professional such as a psychiatrist, psychologist, or other appropriately trained provider. They can discuss your symptoms, determine whether further assessment is appropriate, and help identify possible explanations. If needed, they may refer you to another specialist.

The same difficulty—such as forgetting an appointment, avoiding a task, struggling to concentrate, or feeling restless—can arise from different underlying processes. Looking at the broader pattern is more useful than focusing on one symptom.

ADHD generally has developmental roots and involves persistent difficulties with attention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity.
