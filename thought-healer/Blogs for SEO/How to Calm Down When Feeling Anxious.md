# How to Calm Down When Feeling Anxious: 10 Things You Can Try

Feeling anxious can make everything feel urgent. Your heart may beat faster, your thoughts may race, your muscles may tighten, or you may feel restless, dizzy, overwhelmed, or unable to concentrate.

If you are feeling anxious right now, start small. **Slow and steady your breathing, relax your shoulders and jaw, bring your attention to what is happening around you, and choose one manageable thing to do next.** You do not need to force anxiety to disappear. The goal is to help yourself feel more grounded and give your body and mind a chance to settle.

Anxiety is a normal response to stress, uncertainty, or perceived danger. However, when anxiety becomes persistent, difficult to control, or starts interfering with everyday life, it may be worth talking with a healthcare or mental-health professional.

## How Can I Calm Down When I'm Feeling Anxious?

If you are anxious right now, try these steps one at a time:

1. **Pause and name what you are feeling:** “I am feeling anxious right now.”
    
2. **Slow your breathing:** Breathe gently and regularly instead of taking large, forceful breaths.
    
3. **Release physical tension:** Drop your shoulders, unclench your jaw, and relax your hands.
    
4. **Ground yourself:** Notice what you can see, hear, and physically feel around you.
    
5. **Question the anxious thought:** Ask what you know for certain rather than assuming the worst.
    
6. **Choose one small action:** Focus on the next manageable step instead of solving everything at once.
    

These strategies will not necessarily make anxiety disappear immediately, and different approaches work differently for different people. They can, however, help you respond to anxiety rather than automatically reacting to it.

## 10 Ways to Calm Anxiety

### 1. Try slow, controlled breathing

When you feel anxious, your breathing may become faster or feel uncomfortable. Rather than trying to take huge breaths, aim for breathing that is **gentle, slow, and comfortable**.

Try this:

- Sit or stand somewhere comfortable.
    
- Let your shoulders drop.
    
- Breathe in gently through your nose.
    
- Let the breath flow out slowly.
    
- If comfortable, count slowly from 1 to 5 as you breathe in and again as you breathe out.
    
- Continue for several minutes without forcing the breath.
    

The NHS recommends a similar gentle breathing exercise for stress, anxiety, and panic. The emphasis is on comfortable, regular breathing rather than extreme breath-holding or forceful breathing.

**Why it may help:** Slowing your breathing can give you something steady to focus on and may help your body move away from the feeling of being overwhelmed.

### 2. Use a grounding exercise

Anxiety often pulls your attention toward future possibilities: _What if something goes wrong? What if I can't handle it? What if...?_

Grounding brings your attention back to the present.

Look around and identify:

- 5 things you can see
    
- 4 things you can physically feel
    
- 3 things you can hear
    
- 2 things you can smell
    
- 1 thing you can taste or imagine tasting
    

You do not have to follow this exact sequence. Simply noticing your surroundings can help interrupt a cycle of anxious thinking.

### 3. Relax your muscles

Anxiety can show up in your body before you fully notice it.

Check your:

- jaw
    
- shoulders
    
- hands
    
- neck
    
- stomach
    

Ask yourself: **“Can I soften this area by 10%?”**

You do not need to make your body completely relaxed. Even a small reduction in tension can feel more manageable.

Progressive muscle relaxation, which involves deliberately tensing and relaxing muscle groups, is another relaxation approach that some people find useful.

### 4. Practice mindfulness

Mindfulness does not mean emptying your mind.

Instead, it means paying attention to what is happening right now without immediately judging it or trying to change it.

For example:

> “I'm noticing that my heart is beating quickly. I'm noticing that I'm worried. I'm sitting in a chair. My feet are touching the floor.”

You are observing the experience rather than treating every anxious thought as a fact.

Mindfulness and relaxation techniques are among the approaches that may help with anxiety and stress management.

### 5. Try gentle physical activity

If you are able to move safely, consider taking a short walk, stretching, doing gentle yoga, or simply changing your environment.

Regular physical activity can be part of a broader approach to managing anxiety. The NHS includes activities such as walking, swimming, running, and yoga among things people can try when experiencing anxiety.

You do not need an intense workout. A few minutes of gentle movement may be a more realistic place to start when you already feel overwhelmed.

### 6. Talk to someone you trust

Anxiety can become louder when you keep everything inside.

You could tell someone:

> “I'm feeling pretty anxious right now. I don't need you to fix it. Could you just stay with me for a few minutes?”

You do not necessarily need advice. Sometimes having a supportive person listen can make an anxious moment feel less isolating.

Social support is also part of healthy coping and mental-health care.

### 7. Write down your worries

If your thoughts are going around in circles, put them on paper.

Write:

**What am I worried about?**

Then ask:

**Is this something I can act on today?**

If yes, write down one next step.

If not, write:

**“I don't have to solve this right now.”**

Writing can help turn a vague feeling of overwhelm into something more concrete.

### 8. Reduce excessive caffeine

Caffeine can make some people feel more jittery or physically activated. If you notice that coffee, energy drinks, or other caffeinated products seem to make your anxiety worse, consider reducing your intake.

You do not necessarily need to eliminate caffeine completely. Pay attention to your own pattern.

NIMH includes reducing excessive caffeine and getting adequate sleep among healthy habits that may help reduce anxiety symptoms.

### 9. Create a calming routine

It can be easier to manage anxiety when you have a few familiar strategies ready before you need them.

Your routine might include:

- a regular sleep schedule
    
- a short daily walk
    
- several minutes of breathing practice
    
- regular meals
    
- time away from screens
    
- talking with supportive people
    
- writing down worries
    
- relaxation or mindfulness practice
    

You may need to experiment to find what works best for you.

### 10. Focus on what you can control

Anxiety often asks you to solve an uncertain future.

Instead, ask:

**“What is actually within my control right now?”**

Maybe you can make a phone call, send an email, take a break, prepare for tomorrow, ask for help, or simply get through the next 10 minutes.

You do not need to solve your entire life while feeling anxious.

## A 5-Minute Anxiety-Calming Exercise

If you want something simple to follow right now, try this five-minute reset.

### Minute 1: Notice your surroundings

Look around you.

Notice where you are, what is around you, the temperature of the room, the surface beneath you, and a few sounds you can hear.

There is no need to judge anything. Just observe.

### Minute 2: Slow your breathing

Take gentle breaths.

Breathe in comfortably and allow the breath to leave slowly. Do not force deep breaths or hold your breath for long periods.

If counting helps, count slowly while breathing in and out.

### Minute 3: Release physical tension

Relax your:

- jaw
    
- shoulders
    
- hands
    
- neck
    
- stomach
    

Let your body become slightly less tense.

### Minute 4: Identify the worry

Ask:

**“What am I afraid might happen?”**

Then ask:

**“Is this a fact, or is it a prediction about the future?”**

You do not need to convince yourself that everything will be fine. Simply separate what you know from what you fear might happen.

### Minute 5: Choose one small next action

Ask:

**“What is one useful thing I can do next?”**

It might be drinking some water, stepping outside, sending a message, writing down a task, taking a short walk, or returning to what you were doing.

The purpose of this exercise is **not to force anxiety away**. It is to help you become more grounded and regain some sense of control.

## How to Calm an Anxious Mind

When your mind is anxious, you may feel an urge to solve every possible problem immediately.

That can create a cycle:

**Worry → search for certainty → more questions → more worry.**

Trying to force anxious thoughts to disappear is not always helpful. Instead, try changing your relationship with the thoughts.

### Notice thoughts without automatically believing them

Instead of:

> “Something terrible is going to happen.”

Try:

> “I'm having the thought that something terrible might happen.”

That small difference creates some distance between you and the thought.

### Separate facts from predictions

Ask:

- What do I know for sure?
    
- What am I predicting?
    
- What evidence do I have?
    
- Am I assuming the worst possible outcome?
    
- Is there something useful I can do right now?
    

This approach is consistent with cognitive-behavioral strategies used in anxiety treatment, which can help people recognize and question unhelpful patterns of thinking.

### Put unsolvable problems aside temporarily

Not every problem needs to be solved at 11 p.m.

If there is nothing you can do about a worry tonight, write it down and give yourself permission to return to it later.

This is different from ignoring the problem forever. It simply means recognizing that **right now may not be the best time to solve it**.

## How to Calm Anxiety at Night

Anxiety can feel stronger at night because the day's distractions become quieter. Your mind may suddenly have more space to replay conversations, anticipate tomorrow, or think about unresolved problems.

Try:

- writing down worries before bed
    
- making a predictable wind-down routine
    
- reducing stimulating activities before bedtime
    
- practicing gentle breathing or relaxation
    
- avoiding excessive caffeine, especially if it affects your sleep
    
- keeping your focus on the present rather than trying to solve every future problem
    

If you are lying awake becoming increasingly frustrated, getting out of bed for a quiet activity and returning when you feel sleepier may be more helpful than repeatedly checking the clock or worrying about not sleeping.

These strategies are not a guaranteed cure for insomnia or nighttime anxiety. If anxiety regularly interferes with your sleep, consider discussing it with a healthcare professional.

## How to Calm Anxiety Without Medication

Some people manage anxiety with non-medication approaches, while others need professional treatment that may include medication. There is no failure in needing additional support.

Non-medication approaches can include:

- psychotherapy
    
- cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)
    
- mindfulness
    
- relaxation techniques
    
- physical activity
    
- healthy sleep habits
    
- stress-management skills
    
- social support
    

CBT is a well-studied psychological treatment commonly used for anxiety disorders. Psychotherapy can also help people understand anxiety patterns, develop coping skills, and change behaviors that maintain anxiety.

If you have been prescribed medication, **do not stop or change it without discussing it with your prescriber**.

## What Makes Anxiety Worse?

Several factors can intensify anxiety for some people.

These may include:

- poor or inconsistent sleep
    
- excessive caffeine
    
- ongoing stress
    
- repeatedly checking physical symptoms
    
- constantly seeking reassurance
    
- avoiding more and more situations because they cause anxiety
    
- alcohol or recreational drug use
    
- excessive exposure to distressing content
    
- skipping meals or neglecting basic routines
    

Avoidance deserves particular attention. Avoiding everything that makes you anxious can sometimes provide short-term relief while making the fear harder to face over time. Psychological treatments such as CBT may use gradual, structured approaches to help people respond differently to feared situations.

This does **not** mean you should force yourself into frightening situations without support. If anxiety is severe, a qualified professional can help you approach difficult situations safely and appropriately.

## Why Do I Feel Anxious in the First Place?

Anxiety is part of the body's response to perceived threat, uncertainty, or stress. Feeling anxious occasionally is a normal human experience.

You may feel anxious because of:

- everyday stress
    
- uncertainty about the future
    
- work or school pressures
    
- relationship difficulties
    
- major life changes
    
- difficult or traumatic experiences
    
- lack of sleep
    
- caffeine or other stimulants
    
- ongoing worry
    
- an anxiety disorder
    
- other mental-health or physical factors
    

Sometimes anxiety appears without an obvious conscious trigger. That does not mean the feeling is imaginary or that you are doing something wrong.

If you often find yourself wondering **“Why do I feel anxious for no reason?”**, understanding potential triggers and patterns can be a useful next step.

## What If I Feel Anxious All the Time?

Occasional anxiety is common. But anxiety deserves more attention when it becomes frequent, difficult to control, or begins interfering with everyday life.

You might notice:

- persistent worry
    
- difficulty sleeping
    
- difficulty concentrating
    
- frequent physical symptoms
    
- avoiding places or activities
    
- difficulty completing work or school responsibilities
    
- problems in relationships
    
- significant distress
    

Anxiety disorders involve more than occasional worry or fear and can interfere with work, school, relationships, and daily activities.

You do not have to wait until your anxiety becomes unbearable before seeking help.

## How Long Does Anxiety Last?

There is no single amount of time that anxiety “should” last.

An anxious feeling may settle relatively quickly for one person and remain for much longer for another. How long it lasts can depend on the situation, stress level, physical state, and underlying causes.

The more important question is often:

**“Is anxiety becoming persistent, recurring, difficult to control, or disruptive to my life?”**

If it is, consider speaking with a healthcare or mental-health professional.

---

## Anxiety vs. Panic Attack

Anxiety and panic can overlap, but they are not necessarily the same thing.

**Anxiety** may involve ongoing worry, nervousness, restlessness, tension, or fear related to a current or anticipated situation.

A **panic attack** involves a sudden episode of intense fear or discomfort that can include strong physical symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating, dizziness, trembling, or difficulty breathing.

Not every intense episode of anxiety is a panic attack.

If you frequently experience sudden episodes of intense fear or are changing your life to avoid them, professional assessment can help clarify what is happening.

## When Should I Get Professional Help for Anxiety?

Consider seeking professional support if anxiety:

- persists for weeks or longer
    
- becomes increasingly frequent
    
- feels difficult to control
    
- interferes with everyday activities
    
- regularly disrupts sleep
    
- affects work or school
    
- affects relationships
    
- causes significant avoidance
    
- causes substantial distress
    

You can talk with a:

- therapist
    
- psychologist
    
- psychiatrist
    
- primary-care doctor
    

A professional can help determine what may be contributing to your symptoms and what type of support may be appropriate. Physical symptoms can sometimes have causes unrelated to anxiety, so medical evaluation can also be important when symptoms are new, unusual, or concerning.

You do not need to know whether you have an anxiety disorder before asking for help.

---

## When Anxiety Symptoms Need Urgent Medical Attention

Anxiety can cause physical symptoms, but **not every physical symptom is caused by anxiety**.

Seek urgent medical attention if you experience new or severe symptoms such as:

- severe or new chest pain
    
- severe difficulty breathing
    
- fainting
    
- sudden or unusual physical symptoms
    
- symptoms that feel medically alarming or significantly different from what you have experienced before
    

Do not automatically assume a potentially serious physical symptom is “just anxiety.”

If you are in immediate danger or are thinking about suicide or harming yourself, seek emergency help immediately or go to the nearest emergency department.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. How can I calm down when I feel anxious?

Start with one thing at a time. Slow your breathing, relax your shoulders and jaw, ground yourself by noticing your surroundings, and focus on one manageable next action. You do not need to make the anxiety disappear immediately.

## 2. How do I calm anxiety quickly?

There is no guaranteed instant cure for anxiety. For some people, gentle breathing, grounding, muscle relaxation, and moving to a quieter environment can help them feel more settled.

## 3. What can I do when I feel anxious right now?

Pause, breathe slowly and comfortably, notice what you can see and feel, release physical tension, and ask yourself what you can realistically control right now.

## 4. What breathing exercise helps with anxiety?

Try breathing gently and regularly, such as breathing in comfortably and slowly breathing out. Avoid forcing very deep breaths or prolonged breath-holding. The NHS recommends practicing a comfortable breathing technique for several minutes.

## 5. How can I calm an anxious mind?

Instead of trying to force anxious thoughts away, notice them without automatically believing them. Separate facts from predictions and redirect your attention toward something you can do in the present.

## 6. How do I stop feeling anxious?

You may not be able to stop anxiety immediately, but you can learn ways to respond to it. Breathing exercises, grounding, physical activity, healthy routines, social support, and professional treatment may help depending on the cause and severity.

## 7. How do I calm anxiety at night?

Try writing down worries, following a predictable wind-down routine, practicing relaxation, and reducing stimulating activities. If anxiety repeatedly interferes with sleep, consider talking with a healthcare professional.

## 8. Can exercise help reduce anxiety?

Regular physical activity may help reduce anxiety symptoms for some people. Walking, running, swimming, yoga, and other forms of movement can be part of an overall anxiety-management plan.

## 9. Can anxiety go away on its own?

Sometimes anxiety related to a temporary stressful situation settles as the situation changes. Persistent or recurring anxiety may need additional support, particularly when it interferes with everyday life.

## 10. When should I seek help for anxiety?

Consider professional help when anxiety is persistent, difficult to control, increasingly frequent, or interfering with sleep, work, school, relationships, or everyday activities. You can seek help even if you are unsure whether your symptoms meet the criteria for an anxiety disorder.

# Final Takeaway

When you are feeling anxious, you do not have to solve everything at once.

Start with the next few minutes.

**Slow your breathing. Relax your body. Notice where you are. Question the thought rather than automatically believing it. Then choose one small thing you can control.**

These steps may not eliminate anxiety immediately, but they can give you a more manageable way to respond to it.

And if anxiety keeps returning, becomes difficult to control, or begins interfering with your life, reaching out to a mental-health or healthcare professional is a reasonable next step—not a sign that you have failed to manage it yourself.
