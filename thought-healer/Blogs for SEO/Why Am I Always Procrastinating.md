# Why Am I Always Procrastinating? Common Causes and How to Stop Putting Things Off

If you keep asking yourself, **“Why am I always procrastinating?”**, it can be frustrating—especially when you genuinely want to get things done. You may know exactly what needs to happen, have enough time to do it, and still find yourself checking your phone, cleaning your desk, watching videos, answering unnecessary emails, or doing almost anything except the task in front of you.

Procrastination is common, and it does not automatically mean you're lazy, unmotivated, or lacking discipline. People may procrastinate because a task feels overwhelming, boring, difficult, uncertain, emotionally uncomfortable, or simply too large to start. Stress, anxiety, depression, sleep problems, ADHD, perfectionism, and problems with planning or self-regulation can also contribute.

The important question isn't simply **“Why am I so lazy?”** It is:

**“What is making this task difficult to start or finish?”**

Understanding that difference can make procrastination much easier to address.

---

# What Is Procrastination?

Procrastination means unnecessarily delaying or putting off a task, decision, or action even though you know the delay may create problems later.

For example, you might:

- delay starting an important work project
    
- wait until the night before an assignment is due
    
- repeatedly postpone making an appointment
    
- avoid answering an important message
    
- keep reorganizing your workspace instead of starting
    
- spend an hour scrolling online when you intended to study
    
- repeatedly tell yourself, “I'll do it tomorrow”
    

Procrastination is different from deliberately choosing to postpone something for a good reason.

If you decide to postpone a task because you have a higher-priority responsibility, that isn't necessarily procrastination.

The problem is the pattern of **unhelpful delay despite knowing that starting would be beneficial**.

Research suggests procrastination is a complex behavior involving self-regulation, motivation, emotions, attention, and the characteristics of the task itself. It can also occur alongside psychological difficulties without necessarily being caused by a mental-health disorder.

---

# Why Am I Always Procrastinating?

There isn't one explanation for chronic procrastination.

You may procrastinate because:

- the task feels overwhelming
    
- you're afraid of making a mistake
    
- you want everything to be perfect
    
- you're bored
    
- you're tired
    
- you're anxious
    
- you don't know where to begin
    
- the reward for doing the task feels too far away
    
- you're distracted
    
- you have difficulty managing time
    
- you struggle to regulate attention
    
- you're experiencing low mood
    
- you're mentally overloaded
    
- the task brings up uncomfortable emotions
    

Sometimes procrastination becomes a cycle:

**Task → discomfort → avoidance → temporary relief → more pressure → greater discomfort → more avoidance**

The temporary relief you get from avoiding the task can make avoidance more likely the next time a similar situation occurs.

This is one reason simply telling yourself to “stop procrastinating” may not be enough.

---

# Why Do I Procrastinate Even When I Know I Need to Do Something?

This is one of the most frustrating parts of procrastination.

You may understand the consequences perfectly well:

> “If I don't start today, tomorrow is going to be much harder.”

Yet you still avoid the task.

That doesn't necessarily mean you don't care.

The immediate emotional experience of the task can compete with its long-term importance.

For example, studying for an exam may be important, but opening your notes might immediately bring up boredom, anxiety, uncertainty, or fear of discovering that you don't understand the material.

Scrolling through your phone, on the other hand, may provide immediate entertainment with almost no effort.

Your long-term goal says:

**“Study.”**

Your immediate emotional preference says:

**“Do something easier.”**

Procrastination can occur when the second response wins repeatedly.

---

# 1. The Task Feels Overwhelming

Large, vague tasks are particularly easy to postpone.

“Finish my project” is difficult to act on because it doesn't tell your brain what to do first.

You may keep thinking about the entire project rather than one manageable action.

Try changing:

> “Finish the presentation.”

into:

> “Open the presentation and write the title slide.”

Once you've started, the next step often becomes easier to identify.

---

# 2. You Don't Know Where to Start

Sometimes procrastination is actually an organization problem.

You may have a task but no clear sequence of actions.

For example:

> “I need to get my finances organized.”

That can feel enormous.

Instead, define the first step:

> “Open my bank statement and write down this month's recurring expenses.”

The smaller and more specific the first action is, the less mental effort it may take to begin.

---

# 3. Anxiety Can Fuel Procrastination

Anxiety and procrastination can become closely connected.

You may postpone something because it makes you feel nervous:

- sending an important email
    
- making a phone call
    
- starting an exam
    
- applying for a job
    
- having a difficult conversation
    
- looking at your finances
    
- beginning a complicated project
    

Avoiding the task may reduce anxiety temporarily.

But the task remains unfinished, which can create more anxiety later.

Research has found a meaningful association between procrastination and negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and stress, although these relationships are complex and do not establish that one always causes the other.

If anxiety is a major part of your procrastination, treating the anxiety may be more useful than simply trying to force yourself to work.

---

# 4. Perfectionism Can Lead to Procrastination

You might assume perfectionists always work harder.

Sometimes the opposite happens.

If you believe your work needs to be excellent, starting can feel threatening.

You may think:

- “What if this isn't good enough?”
    
- “I need to know exactly what I'm doing before I start.”
    
- “I'll begin when I have more time.”
    
- “I need the perfect idea first.”
    

The result can be avoidance.

One useful strategy is to separate **starting** from **finishing perfectly**.

Give yourself permission to create a rough first version.

You can improve a draft.

You can't improve something you never started.

---

# 5. Boredom and Lack of Interest

Some tasks are simply boring.

Administrative work, repetitive paperwork, routine studying, cleaning, or tedious reports may not provide much immediate reward.

When a task is uninteresting, your attention may naturally drift toward something more stimulating.

This doesn't mean boredom is a disorder.

Instead, consider whether you can make the task more active.

For example:

- listen to appropriate background audio
    
- work alongside someone
    
- divide the task into smaller challenges
    
- alternate repetitive work with more engaging activities
    
- give yourself a clearly defined stopping point
    

---

# 6. Stress and Mental Overload

When you're already carrying too many responsibilities, even a relatively small task can feel impossible to start.

You might be thinking about:

- work
    
- money
    
- relationships
    
- family responsibilities
    
- deadlines
    
- health
    
- unfinished tasks
    

Your brain is essentially trying to manage multiple competing priorities.

The result can look like procrastination even when the deeper issue is overload.

If everything feels urgent, identify what actually matters most today.

You don't need to solve your entire life before beginning one task.

---

# 7. Poor Sleep and Fatigue

Being exhausted makes many tasks harder.

When you're tired, you may have less mental energy for planning, decision-making, sustained attention, and self-control.

This can create a particularly frustrating cycle:

**Poor sleep → less energy → procrastination → late-night work → less sleep → more procrastination**

Bedtime procrastination is one example. Research has linked voluntarily delaying bedtime with shorter sleep duration, poorer sleep quality, and greater daytime fatigue.

If you're regularly exhausted, the answer may not be another productivity technique.

You may need to address the sleep problem itself.

---

# 8. ADHD Can Contribute to Procrastination

ADHD can involve difficulties with attention regulation, organization, time management, working memory, task initiation, and completing tasks.

For some people with ADHD, procrastination isn't simply a matter of not wanting to work. Getting started may require unusually high mental effort, especially when a task is boring, repetitive, poorly structured, or doesn't provide immediate stimulation.

Recent systematic-review evidence found a consistent association between ADHD symptoms and procrastination, with inattention showing the strongest relationship among the factors studied. However, the research does **not** establish that ADHD causes procrastination in every individual.

Most importantly:

**Procrastination alone does not mean you have ADHD.**

Many people without ADHD procrastinate.

If procrastination occurs alongside longstanding difficulties with attention, organization, forgetfulness, impulsivity, time management, and task completion, an ADHD assessment may be worth discussing with a qualified professional.

---

# 9. Depression and Low Mood

Depression can affect energy, motivation, concentration, decision-making, and interest in everyday activities.

If you're experiencing depression, tasks that once felt manageable may suddenly feel exhausting.

You may think:

> “I know I need to do it, but I just can't make myself start.”

This isn't necessarily ordinary procrastination.

If delaying tasks occurs alongside persistent low mood, loss of interest, significant fatigue, changes in sleep or appetite, hopelessness, or other depressive symptoms, consider speaking with a mental-health professional.

At the same time, procrastination by itself does not diagnose depression.

---

# 10. Fear of Failure

Sometimes procrastination protects you from confronting the possibility of failure.

If you don't start, you don't have to discover whether you can succeed.

This can happen with:

- exams
    
- job applications
    
- presentations
    
- creative work
    
- business projects
    
- difficult conversations
    

The mind may find avoidance emotionally safer than taking the risk.

A useful shift is to focus on **what you can control**.

Instead of:

> “I have to succeed.”

try:

> “My job right now is to complete the next step.”

---

# 11. Fear of Success or Change

Less obviously, people may sometimes delay tasks because completing them would create change.

For example, finishing a project might lead to:

- more responsibility
    
- a difficult decision
    
- increased expectations
    
- a new role
    
- uncertainty about what happens next
    

This isn't always conscious.

If you repeatedly procrastinate at moments when completing a task would lead to a major change, it may be useful to explore what you feel about that change.

---

# 12. You Are Relying on Motivation to Appear First

A common misconception is:

> “I'll start when I feel motivated.”

Unfortunately, motivation often doesn't arrive before action.

Sometimes action comes first.

You start for five minutes, gain some momentum, understand the task better, and then feel more willing to continue.

This doesn't mean you should force yourself through severe exhaustion or distress.

It means you don't always have to wait for the perfect emotional state before beginning.

---

# Why Am I Procrastinating on Things I Actually Enjoy?

Procrastination isn't limited to unpleasant tasks.

You might postpone:

- exercising
    
- reading
    
- hobbies
    
- creative projects
    
- meeting friends
    
- personal goals
    

This can happen when the task requires initiation, planning, or switching away from your current activity—even when you expect to enjoy it once you begin.

If this happens across many areas of life and is accompanied by significant difficulty with attention, organization, motivation, or daily functioning, it may be worth discussing the broader pattern with a professional.

---

# Why Do I Procrastinate Until the Last Minute?

Some people repeatedly wait until a deadline becomes urgent.

The approaching deadline creates:

- pressure
    
- adrenaline
    
- a clear goal
    
- immediate consequences
    
- fewer competing choices
    

That sudden urgency can make it easier to focus.

You may even feel that you “work better under pressure.”

But regularly depending on last-minute urgency can increase stress, reduce available time, interfere with sleep, and leave less room for errors or unexpected problems.

If deadlines are the only thing that reliably gets you started, consider creating smaller artificial deadlines along the way.

---

# Why Do I Procrastinate at Work?

Work procrastination can have many causes.

You may be dealing with:

- unclear priorities
    
- a large project
    
- repetitive tasks
    
- workplace stress
    
- perfectionism
    
- difficult coworkers
    
- fear of making mistakes
    
- frequent interruptions
    
- excessive meetings
    
- email overload
    
- burnout or exhaustion
    
- difficulty managing competing demands
    

Try asking:

**“What exactly am I avoiding?”**

If the answer is “the whole project,” make the first action smaller.

If the answer is “sending the email because I'm worried about their response,” the underlying problem may be anxiety rather than productivity.

If the answer is “I genuinely don't know what my priority is,” you may need clarification rather than greater discipline.

---

# Why Do I Procrastinate While Studying?

Students often procrastinate when studying feels difficult, boring, overwhelming, or emotionally threatening.

Common triggers include:

- fear of failing
    
- perfectionism
    
- difficult material
    
- poor sleep
    
- phone distractions
    
- unrealistic study plans
    
- uncertainty about where to begin
    
- long study sessions
    
- exam anxiety
    

Instead of:

> “I need to study for four hours.”

try:

> “For the next 25 minutes, I'll work through five practice questions.”

You can also use active study methods such as practice questions, retrieval from memory, explaining concepts in your own words, and spaced review.

The goal is not to study continuously. It's to make starting and returning to the material manageable.

---

# Why Does Procrastination Make Me Feel Worse?

Procrastination can provide short-term relief while creating longer-term stress.

You avoid a difficult task and immediately feel better.

Then the deadline gets closer.

You remember the unfinished task.

You feel guilty.

You become more anxious.

The task feels even harder.

Then you avoid it again.

This creates a cycle:

**Avoidance → temporary relief → increased pressure → negative emotions → more avoidance**

Research has found a positive association between procrastination and negative emotions, including anxiety, depression, and stress. However, the relationship can work in more than one direction, and procrastination should not be treated as proof of a mental-health condition.

Breaking the cycle often starts with taking one small action rather than waiting until you feel completely ready.

---

# ADHD vs Anxiety vs Stress vs Depression: How Can They Relate to Procrastination?

Several different experiences can look like procrastination.

|Possible contributor|How it may affect procrastination|
|---|---|
|**ADHD**|Difficulty with task initiation, attention regulation, organization, time management, working memory, and sustaining effort|
|**Anxiety**|Avoiding tasks that trigger worry, uncertainty, fear, or uncomfortable emotions|
|**Stress**|Mental overload can make prioritizing and starting tasks more difficult|
|**Depression**|Low energy, motivation, concentration, and interest can make ordinary tasks harder|
|**Perfectionism**|Fear that the result won't be good enough can delay starting|
|**Fatigue**|Reduced mental energy can make effortful tasks feel disproportionately difficult|

These patterns can overlap.

For example, someone might have ADHD-related difficulty starting a task and then experience anxiety because the deadline is approaching.

Another person might procrastinate primarily because they are afraid of making a mistake.

Someone else may simply be exhausted.

**Procrastination cannot tell you which explanation applies to you.**

---

# Is Procrastination a Mental-Health Disorder?

No. Procrastination itself is not automatically a mental-health disorder.

It is a behavior that can occur in many different circumstances.

Occasional procrastination is normal.

It becomes more concerning when it is persistent, causes significant distress, repeatedly interferes with important responsibilities, or occurs alongside other symptoms that may indicate an underlying problem.

Psychological treatments can help some people reduce problematic procrastination. A systematic review and meta-analysis found small overall benefits from psychological interventions, with some evidence of a moderate benefit for cognitive behavioral therapy, although the available studies were limited and varied in quality.

# How to Stop Procrastinating

You don't need to completely eliminate procrastination from your life.

The goal is to make it easier to recognize the pattern and take useful action.

## 1. Identify What You're Avoiding

Ask:

**“What feels uncomfortable about this task?”**

Is it:

- boredom?
    
- uncertainty?
    
- fear of failure?
    
- confusion?
    
- anxiety?
    
- perfectionism?
    
- exhaustion?
    

The answer can determine the strategy you use.

---

## 2. Make the First Step Ridiculously Specific

Don't write:

> “Work on taxes.”

Write:

> “Open the tax folder.”

Don't write:

> “Study chemistry.”

Write:

> “Read the first two pages of chapter three.”

The goal is to remove ambiguity.

---

## 3. Use the Five-Minute Rule

Tell yourself:

> “I only have to do this for five minutes.”

After five minutes, you can decide whether to continue.

The purpose isn't to trick yourself into completing everything.

It is to lower the psychological barrier to starting.

---

## 4. Reduce Friction

Make the desired action easier.

If you want to exercise:

- put your clothes out beforehand
    
- keep your shoes accessible
    
- decide what workout you'll do
    

If you want to study:

- open the material
    
- prepare your desk
    
- remove unnecessary notifications
    

If you want to write:

- open the document
    
- write one rough sentence
    

Small preparation can reduce the number of decisions required to begin.

---

## 5. Remove Immediate Distractions

If your phone is beside you, you're asking yourself to resist it repeatedly.

Instead:

- silence notifications
    
- put the phone away
    
- close unnecessary browser tabs
    
- use a distraction-reduced workspace
    
- keep only the materials for the current task visible
    

Don't depend entirely on willpower when you can change the environment.

---

## 6. Break Large Projects Into Milestones

Instead of:

**“Complete the project.”**

Try:

1. Define the goal.
    
2. Gather the information.
    
3. Create an outline.
    
4. Write the first section.
    
5. Review it.
    
6. Complete the remaining sections.
    
7. Proofread.
    

A project becomes less intimidating when its structure is visible.

---

## 7. Stop Waiting for Perfect Conditions

You may never feel completely ready.

You don't need perfect motivation, a perfect desk, unlimited time, or complete confidence to begin.

Ask:

**“What could I reasonably do with the time and energy I have today?”**

---

## 8. Use Short, Realistic Work Periods

Experiment with focused periods followed by breaks.

You might start with:

**20 minutes of work → 5-minute break**

or:

**30 minutes of work → 5–10-minute break**

There is no universally correct interval.

Choose something that feels challenging but realistic.

---

## 9. Use External Deadlines

If you repeatedly postpone a task until the last moment, create smaller deadlines.

For example:

- outline by Monday
    
- first draft by Wednesday
    
- revision by Friday
    

This distributes the workload instead of creating one overwhelming deadline.

---

## 10. Reward Completion, Not Just Intention

Give yourself something pleasant after completing a meaningful step.

For example:

> “After I finish these three pages, I'll make coffee.”

The reward doesn't need to be elaborate.

The point is to make completion feel more immediate.

---

# A 5-Minute Anti-Procrastination Reset

When you notice yourself avoiding something, try this.

### Minute 1: Stop

Put down the phone or close the distracting website.

Take a moment to notice:

**“I'm avoiding something.”**

Don't criticize yourself.

### Minute 2: Identify the task

Write down exactly what you're supposed to do.

Not:

> “Fix my life.”

Instead:

> “Reply to the email from my manager.”

### Minute 3: Identify the barrier

Ask:

**“What makes me want to avoid this?”**

Maybe you're tired.

Maybe you're worried.

Maybe you don't know what to write.

Maybe the task feels enormous.

### Minute 4: Shrink the task

Choose the smallest useful action.

For example:

> “Open the email and write the first sentence.”

### Minute 5: Begin

Work on that action for five minutes.

You don't have to finish everything.

The objective is simply to interrupt the avoidance cycle.

---

# What Not to Do When You're Procrastinating

Try to avoid responding to procrastination with:

### “I'm just lazy.”

This turns a behavior into an identity.

### “I'll start when I feel motivated.”

Motivation may increase after you begin.

### “I need to finish everything today.”

This can make large tasks even more overwhelming.

### “I'll just take a quick break.”

If the break consistently becomes an hour of scrolling, change the environment rather than relying on intention.

### “I must work harder.”

If the real issue is anxiety, depression, ADHD, poor sleep, or overwhelming demands, simply increasing effort may not solve it.

### “I must have ADHD.”

Procrastination alone cannot establish an ADHD diagnosis.

---

# How to Track Your Procrastination

You don't need to monitor yourself constantly.

For a few days, simply note patterns such as:

- What task did I avoid?
    
- What time of day was it?
    
- How much sleep did I get?
    
- What was I feeling?
    
- What did I do instead?
    
- How difficult did the task seem?
    
- Did I eventually complete it?
    
- What helped me start?
    

You may discover a pattern.

For example:

**“I procrastinate most when tasks are vague.”**

Or:

**“I avoid tasks that involve possible criticism.”**

Or:

**“I procrastinate much more when I've slept badly.”**

That information can be more useful than simply telling yourself that you need more discipline.

# When Is Procrastination Normal?

Almost everyone postpones things sometimes.

It can happen when you're:

- tired
    
- distracted
    
- stressed
    
- bored
    
- busy
    
- uncertain
    
- emotionally upset
    
- dealing with too many responsibilities
    

Occasional procrastination isn't necessarily a problem.

The bigger concern is a persistent pattern that repeatedly prevents you from doing things that matter to you.

---

# When Should I Be Concerned About Procrastination?

Consider speaking with a professional if procrastination:

- consistently interferes with work or education
    
- causes significant distress
    
- damages important relationships
    
- leads to repeated missed deadlines
    
- affects financial or personal responsibilities
    
- occurs across many areas of life
    
- has become significantly worse
    
- occurs alongside persistent anxiety or low mood
    
- occurs alongside longstanding attention or organization difficulties
    
- continues despite reasonable changes to your routine
    

You don't have to wait until procrastination becomes severe before asking for help.

---

# When to Talk to a Mental-Health Professional

Consider speaking with a psychologist, psychiatrist, counselor, or other qualified mental-health professional if procrastination occurs alongside:

- persistent anxiety
    
- excessive worry
    
- depression or low mood
    
- loss of interest
    
- severe perfectionism
    
- significant emotional distress
    
- longstanding attention difficulties
    
- problems with organization or time management
    
- difficulty functioning at work, school, or home
    

A professional can help determine whether procrastination is primarily a behavioral pattern or whether another issue may be contributing.

If ADHD is a concern, an evaluation can consider the broader pattern rather than focusing only on procrastination.

---

# When to Talk to a Doctor

A doctor may be appropriate if procrastination is accompanied by unexplained or persistent:

- fatigue
    
- sleep problems
    
- changes in mood
    
- cognitive difficulties
    
- medication-related concerns
    
- other physical symptoms
    

Some medical issues and medications can affect energy, alertness, sleep, or cognitive functioning.

Don't stop or change prescribed medication on your own. If you suspect a medication is contributing to changes in motivation, alertness, or functioning, discuss it with your doctor or pharmacist.

---

# What If Procrastination Is Making Me Feel Hopeless?

Persistent procrastination can be emotionally exhausting.

You may feel ashamed, frustrated, guilty, or convinced that you're incapable of changing.

Try to separate **the behavior from your identity**.

Instead of:

> “I'm a procrastinator.”

try:

> “I'm having difficulty starting this task.”

That distinction matters.

A behavior can change.

If procrastination is accompanied by severe emotional distress, hopelessness, or thoughts of harming yourself, seek urgent help from a qualified mental-health professional or local emergency service rather than trying to handle the situation alone.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. Why am I always procrastinating?

You may procrastinate because tasks feel overwhelming, boring, uncertain, emotionally uncomfortable, or difficult to start. Stress, anxiety, perfectionism, fatigue, depression, ADHD-related attention difficulties, and poor task organization can also contribute. Procrastination itself does not mean you're lazy or that you have a mental-health disorder.

## 2. Why do I procrastinate even when I know I need to do something?

Knowing that a task is important doesn't eliminate the discomfort associated with starting it. Anxiety, boredom, uncertainty, perfectionism, fatigue, or feeling overwhelmed can make immediate avoidance feel easier. Breaking the task into a very small first action can reduce that barrier.

## 3. Is procrastination a sign of ADHD?

Procrastination can occur in people with ADHD, particularly when they have difficulties with attention regulation, organization, time management, and task initiation. However, procrastination alone is not a sign that you have ADHD. A proper assessment considers a broader, persistent pattern of symptoms and their impact on daily functioning.

## 4. Can anxiety cause procrastination?

Anxiety can contribute to procrastination, particularly when a task creates worry, uncertainty, fear of failure, or fear of judgment. Avoiding the task may temporarily reduce anxiety, but the unfinished task can create more pressure later. Procrastination and anxiety are associated, but one does not necessarily cause the other.

## 5. Can depression make you procrastinate?

Depression can make tasks harder to start because it may involve low energy, reduced motivation, difficulty concentrating, loss of interest, and slowed thinking. However, procrastination by itself does not indicate depression. If delaying tasks occurs alongside persistent low mood or other depressive symptoms, consider speaking with a mental-health professional.

## 6. Why do I procrastinate until the last minute?

A deadline can create urgency and temporarily make it easier to focus. Some people therefore become accustomed to relying on last-minute pressure. However, this can increase stress and leave less time to handle mistakes or unexpected problems. Creating smaller deadlines before the final deadline may help.

## 7. How can I stop procrastinating?

Start by identifying what you're avoiding and why. Then make the first step very small and specific, remove distractions, use a realistic work period, and focus on starting rather than finishing everything. If procrastination is persistent or significantly impairing, professional support may help identify underlying contributors.

## 8. Is procrastination caused by laziness?

Not necessarily. Procrastination can involve emotional avoidance, difficulty initiating tasks, anxiety, perfectionism, fatigue, boredom, attention difficulties, or feeling overwhelmed. Calling yourself lazy doesn't explain why the behavior is happening. Identifying the barrier is usually more useful than criticizing yourself.

## 9. Why do I procrastinate even on things I enjoy?

Even enjoyable activities may require planning, initiation, or switching away from whatever you're currently doing. Difficulty starting enjoyable activities can also occur when you're tired, overwhelmed, experiencing low mood, or having broader executive-function difficulties. Look for whether the pattern occurs across many types of activities.

## 10. When should I get help for procrastination?

Consider professional help when procrastination repeatedly interferes with work, education, relationships, finances, or daily responsibilities; causes significant distress; or occurs alongside anxiety, depression, longstanding attention difficulties, severe fatigue, or other concerning symptoms. A professional can help determine what is contributing and what type of support may be appropriate.

# Editorial Takeaway

The most important message for readers is that **procrastination is a behavior, not a character flaw**.

Sometimes the reason is straightforward: the task is boring, unclear, or too large.

Sometimes procrastination is connected to fear, perfectionism, anxiety, stress, fatigue, low mood, or difficulty regulating attention.

And sometimes several factors are operating at the same time.

Rather than asking:

> **“Why am I so lazy?”**

encourage readers to ask:

> **“What is making this task difficult to start?”**

That question turns procrastination from a judgment about who they are into a problem that can be understood and worked on.

Small steps matter. You don't need to become perfectly productive. You need to make the next useful action easier to take.
