# What Should I Talk About in Therapy? A Beginner’s Guide to Opening Up

If you're thinking about starting therapy, you may have an unexpected question:

**“What am I actually supposed to talk about?”**

You don't need to arrive with a perfectly organized list of problems. You also don't need to have a diagnosis, know exactly what's wrong, or prepare an impressive explanation of your life.

Therapy is a place to talk about **what is bothering you, what you are experiencing, what you want to change, and anything that seems relevant to understanding your situation**.

You might talk about anxiety, sadness, relationships, anger, overthinking, intrusive thoughts, work, family, sleep, self-esteem, grief, trauma, or a difficult decision. You can also say, **“I don't know what to talk about today.”** Your therapist can help guide the conversation.

The most useful starting point is often surprisingly simple:

**Talk about what brought you to therapy and what you hope will be different.**

# What Should You Talk About in Therapy?

The simplest answer is:

**Talk about what matters to you.**

Therapy is not an exam where there are correct answers.

You can talk about:

- Why you decided to seek therapy
    
- What you've been feeling
    
- Thoughts that keep bothering you
    
- Problems in relationships
    
- Stress at work or school
    
- Family difficulties
    
- Sleep problems
    
- Anxiety
    
- Low mood
    
- Anger
    
- Grief
    
- Self-esteem
    
- Habits you want to change
    
- Difficult memories
    
- Things you avoid
    
- Fears
    
- Major life changes
    
- Things you don't understand about yourself
    
- Goals for the future
    

Psychotherapy is designed to help people identify and address troubling emotions, thoughts, and behaviors, although the specific focus varies by therapy approach and individual needs. ([nimh.nih.gov](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/psychotherapies?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

# Start With Why You're There

If you don't know what to say, begin with the reason you made the appointment.

For example:

> “I've been feeling anxious for the last few months.”

Or:

> “I can't stop overthinking everything.”

Or:

> “My relationship is causing me a lot of stress.”

Or:

> “I don't really know what's wrong, but I don't feel like myself.”

Or simply:

> “I thought therapy might help, but I'm not sure where to start.”

All of these are good starting points.

Your therapist can ask follow-up questions to understand what's happening.

# 15 Things You Can Talk About in Therapy

## 1. What has been bothering you lately

Start with what's taking up the most mental or emotional space.

Ask yourself:

**“What is the thing I keep thinking about?”**

It could be a relationship, work, health, family, money, loneliness, or something that happened years ago.

You don't have to decide whether it's important enough.

If it is bothering you, it's worth mentioning.

---

## 2. Your emotions

Sometimes people know they're struggling but can't identify exactly why.

You can talk about feeling:

- Sad
    
- Angry
    
- Anxious
    
- Lonely
    
- Irritable
    
- Guilty
    
- Ashamed
    
- Numb
    
- Overwhelmed
    
- Empty
    
- Confused
    
- Frustrated
    

You don't need to explain the emotion perfectly.

You can simply say:

> “I don't understand why I feel this way.”

That itself is useful information.

---

## 3. Thoughts you can't stop having

If your mind keeps returning to the same thought, tell your therapist.

For example:

> “I keep replaying conversations.”

> “I can't stop worrying about the future.”

> “I constantly think about something embarrassing I did.”

> “I have thoughts that scare me.”

> “I keep analyzing whether I made the right decision.”

These patterns can help your therapist understand what's happening and determine what strategies may be appropriate.

---

## 4. Intrusive thoughts

If you experience unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that disturb you, you can talk about them in therapy.

This can be difficult because you may feel embarrassed or afraid that the therapist will judge you.

Mental-health professionals regularly work with people experiencing distressing thoughts.

You can start by saying:

> “I have some thoughts that I'm uncomfortable talking about.”

You don't have to immediately describe every detail.

If intrusive thoughts are accompanied by compulsions, reassurance-seeking, checking, avoidance, or significant distress, mention that too. These details can be important when assessing whether OCD or another difficulty may be involved. ([nimh.nih.gov](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-when-unwanted-thoughts-or-repetitive-behaviors-take-over?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

---

## 5. Things you're avoiding

Avoidance can be important information.

Tell your therapist if you're avoiding:

- Social situations
    
- Certain people
    
- Work
    
- School
    
- Driving
    
- Phone calls
    
- Difficult conversations
    
- Specific places
    
- Certain thoughts or memories
    

You might think:

> “It's embarrassing that I avoid this.”

But avoidance can be an important part of understanding anxiety and other difficulties.

---

## 6. Your relationships

Relationships often provide important clues about emotional patterns.

You can talk about:

- Arguments
    
- Communication problems
    
- Trust
    
- Jealousy
    
- Boundaries
    
- Family conflict
    
- Dating
    
- Breakups
    
- Fear of abandonment
    
- Difficulty becoming close to people
    
- Feeling misunderstood
    
- People-pleasing
    
- Loneliness
    

You don't have to bring your partner or family member to discuss relationship issues individually.

---

## 7. Your work or studies

Work and education can affect mental health significantly.

Talk about:

- Burnout
    
- Procrastination
    
- Difficulty concentrating
    
- Perfectionism
    
- Workplace conflict
    
- Academic pressure
    
- Fear of failure
    
- Motivation
    
- Time management
    
- Career uncertainty
    

If your difficulties are affecting your ability to function, tell your therapist specifically how.

---

## 8. Sleep

Sleep is important information for a therapist.

Tell them if you've noticed:

- Difficulty falling asleep
    
- Waking frequently
    
- Racing thoughts at night
    
- Sleeping excessively
    
- Waking exhausted
    
- Changes in your sleep schedule
    

Sleep changes can occur alongside many psychological and physical difficulties, so your therapist may want to understand the pattern.

---

## 9. Your habits and behaviors

Sometimes the most useful information isn't about what you think.

It's about what you do.

You might discuss:

- Procrastination
    
- Avoidance
    
- Excessive checking
    
- Reassurance-seeking
    
- Overworking
    
- Social withdrawal
    
- Emotional eating
    
- Difficulty maintaining routines
    
- Repeated arguments
    
- Compulsive behaviors
    

Don't worry about making yourself look good.

Therapy works better when your therapist has an accurate picture of what's actually happening.

---

## 10. Difficult experiences from your past

Past experiences may be relevant to current difficulties.

These might include:

- Childhood experiences
    
- Family conflict
    
- Loss
    
- Bullying
    
- Abuse
    
- Trauma
    
- Relationship experiences
    
- Major failures
    
- Rejection
    

But you don't have to tell your entire history immediately.

If something feels too difficult to discuss, you can say:

> “There's something in my past that I think might be relevant, but I'm not ready to talk about it yet.”

That is completely reasonable.

---

## 11. Things you're ashamed of

Shame can make people hide the exact things they most need help with.

You might worry:

> “What will my therapist think of me?”

But hiding an important part of the problem can make it harder for your therapist to understand what's happening.

You don't need to reveal everything at once.

You can start by saying:

> “There's something I'm embarrassed to tell you.”

That gives you a way into the conversation without having to immediately explain everything.

---

## 12. Things you want to change

Therapy becomes more focused when you know what you want to be different.

Your goal might be:

- “I want to stop overthinking.”
    
- “I want to feel less anxious.”
    
- “I want to communicate better.”
    
- “I want to stop avoiding people.”
    
- “I want to understand why I keep doing this.”
    
- “I want to feel more confident.”
    
- “I want to sleep better.”
    
- “I want to manage my anger.”
    
- “I want to stop being so hard on myself.”
    

Your goals don't have to be perfectly defined.

You can develop them together with your therapist.

---

## 13. Things that aren't working

Tell your therapist what you've already tried.

For example:

> “I've tried distracting myself, but the thoughts keep returning.”

> “I've tried talking to my friends, but I still feel stuck.”

> “I've tried meditation, but I'm still struggling.”

This information can help your therapist understand which strategies have and haven't been useful.

---

## 14. Changes since your last session

If you're already attending therapy, don't feel that every session needs a completely new topic.

You can discuss:

- Something that happened during the week
    
- A difficult conversation
    
- A setback
    
- A new realization
    
- Something that made you feel better
    
- Something that made symptoms worse
    
- A therapy exercise that was difficult
    
- A situation where you used a new skill
    

Therapy often involves connecting what happens in sessions with what happens in everyday life.

---

## 15. The therapy itself

This is one of the most important—and most overlooked—topics.

You can talk about your experience with therapy.

For example:

> “I don't understand why we're doing this exercise.”

> “I don't feel like I'm making progress.”

> “I feel uncomfortable talking about this.”

> “I don't think this approach is working for me.”

> “I feel like I need more structure.”

> “I feel judged.”

You should be able to discuss concerns about the therapeutic process.

# What Should I Say in My First Therapy Session?

You don't need a speech.

A simple structure can help:

### 1. What brought me here?

> “I've been struggling with anxiety.”

### 2. How long has it been happening?

> “It's been getting worse over the last few months.”

### 3. How is it affecting my life?

> “I'm having trouble sleeping and concentrating.”

### 4. What do I want to change?

> “I want to feel more in control of my thoughts.”

That's enough to start.

The therapist can then ask questions to understand the situation in more detail.

---

# What If I Don't Know What to Talk About?

This happens.

You can literally say:

> **“I don't know what to talk about today.”**

Your therapist can help generate a direction.

You might also ask:

> “Can we review what we've been working on?”

or:

> “I feel like I have a lot going on, but I don't know what is most important.”

Another useful approach is to ask yourself:

**“What has affected me the most since our last session?”**

That often provides a starting point.

---

# Should I Prepare for Therapy?

You don't have to do extensive preparation.

But writing down a few things beforehand can make the session easier.

Consider noting:

### What am I experiencing?

For example:

- Anxiety
    
- Low mood
    
- Anger
    
- Overthinking
    
- Intrusive thoughts
    
- Sleep problems
    

### When did it start?

Try to estimate when you first noticed the problem.

### How often does it happen?

Daily? Weekly? Occasionally?

### What triggers it?

Think about situations, thoughts, people, places, or circumstances that make it worse.

### What helps?

Notice anything that reduces the problem, even temporarily.

### How is it affecting my life?

Consider:

- Work
    
- Studies
    
- Relationships
    
- Sleep
    
- Social life
    
- Daily responsibilities
    

### What do I want from therapy?

You can write one sentence.

For example:

> “I want to understand why I keep overthinking and learn how to manage it.”

# What If I'm Afraid of Being Judged?

This is one of the biggest barriers to opening up.

You may worry that your therapist will think:

- You're strange
    
- You're weak
    
- You're a bad person
    
- You're overreacting
    
- Your problem isn't serious enough
    
- You should be able to handle it yourself
    

Remember that therapy is specifically designed to provide a professional setting for discussing difficult thoughts, emotions, and behaviors.

You can also tell your therapist:

> “I'm worried you'll judge me.”

That itself can become a useful part of the session.

---

# Do I Have to Tell My Therapist Everything?

**No.**

You control the pace at which you disclose personal information.

You can say:

> “I'm not ready to talk about that yet.”

However, it's useful to be as honest as you reasonably can, particularly about symptoms and behaviors that affect your safety or treatment.

If you're unsure whether something is relevant, you can simply ask:

> “Would it be useful for you to know about this?”

---

# What If I Lie to My Therapist?

People sometimes minimize problems because they're embarrassed, afraid, or unsure how the therapist will react.

For example:

> “My anxiety isn't that bad.”

when actually it is severely affecting their sleep and daily life.

If you notice yourself hiding or minimizing something, consider telling your therapist that you're doing so.

You could say:

> “I'm having trouble being completely honest because I'm embarrassed.”

That is often more useful than pretending everything is fine.

---

# Should I Talk About Things I Think Are Unimportant?

Possibly.

A seemingly minor detail can sometimes help your therapist understand a larger pattern.

For example, saying:

> “I get unusually anxious when someone doesn't respond to my message.”

might reveal something important about uncertainty, rejection, relationships, or anxiety.

You don't have to decide whether something is clinically important.

Share it and let the therapist help determine its relevance.

---

# What If I Cry or Get Emotional?

That's okay.

Therapy can involve difficult emotions.

You don't need to apologize for crying, becoming quiet, or needing a moment.

You can say:

> “This is difficult for me to talk about.”

Your therapist can slow down or help you manage the moment.

You also don't have to cry for therapy to be meaningful.

---

# What If I Get Angry at My Therapist?

You can talk about that too.

Maybe you felt misunderstood.

Maybe you disagreed with something they said.

Maybe you felt they weren't listening.

Rather than automatically leaving, consider discussing it if you feel safe doing so.

How the therapist responds can tell you something important about whether the therapeutic relationship is working.

---

# What If I Don't Agree With My Therapist?

You are allowed to disagree.

Therapy is collaborative.

You can ask:

> “Why do you think that?”

> “What makes you believe that?”

> “Can we approach this differently?”

A qualified therapist should be able to explain the reasoning behind treatment recommendations and discuss alternatives where appropriate.

---

# Should I Talk About My Therapist's Role in My Life?

Yes.

The relationship between therapist and client can itself become relevant to therapy.

For example, you might notice:

- You're afraid of disappointing the therapist
    
- You desperately want their approval
    
- You feel angry when they challenge you
    
- You don't want sessions to end
    
- You feel uncomfortable with silence
    
- You worry they think badly of you
    

These reactions can be worth discussing rather than hiding.

---

# What If I Have Thoughts of Suicide or Self-Harm?

This is something you should tell your therapist.

You don't have to protect your therapist from difficult information.

If you're experiencing thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or harming someone else, tell your mental-health professional directly.

If you believe you may act on those thoughts or cannot keep yourself safe, seek urgent help through your local emergency service, emergency department, or an appropriate crisis service rather than waiting for a routine therapy appointment.

---

# What If I Have Intrusive Thoughts That Scare Me?

Tell your therapist, especially if the thoughts are frequent, distressing, or causing you to check, avoid, seek reassurance, or perform mental rituals.

You can start without giving every detail:

> “I have unwanted thoughts that really scare me, and I'm worried about what they mean.”

That gives the therapist an opportunity to ask appropriate questions.

Remember that **an unwanted thought does not automatically represent an intention or desire**.

---

# Therapy Doesn't Have to Be Perfectly Organized

Your thoughts probably aren't organized.

That's okay.

You don't need to walk into therapy with:

- A diagnosis
    
- A perfect timeline
    
- The correct terminology
    
- A list of symptoms
    
- A clear explanation
    
- A five-year treatment plan
    

You can bring confusion.

You can bring uncertainty.

You can bring a messy story.

**That's part of what therapy is for.**

# Common Mistakes People Make in Therapy

## Trying to say what the therapist wants to hear

Therapy is more useful when you are honest rather than trying to give the “right” answers.

## Only discussing symptoms

Symptoms matter, but your relationships, environment, goals, and experiences can also be important.

## Hiding setbacks

A difficult week doesn't mean you've failed. Tell your therapist.

## Pretending you're okay

If you're struggling, say so.

## Waiting for the therapist to do all the talking

Therapy is generally collaborative. Your participation matters.

## Avoiding the most important topic

Sometimes the topic you're most reluctant to discuss is the one causing the most distress.

You don't have to discuss it immediately, but you can tell your therapist that it exists.

# 10 Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. What should I talk about in therapy?

Talk about what is affecting your thoughts, emotions, behaviors, relationships, or everyday life. You can discuss anxiety, depression, stress, relationships, intrusive thoughts, difficult experiences, sleep, work, habits, fears, or personal goals. If you're unsure where to start, simply explain why you decided to seek therapy.

## 2. What should I say to my therapist in the first session?

Start with what brought you to therapy, how long you've been struggling, how the problem affects your life, and what you hope will change. You don't need a prepared speech. Saying “I'm not sure where to start” is perfectly acceptable and gives the therapist an opportunity to guide the conversation.

## 3. What if I don't know what to talk about in therapy?

Tell your therapist that you don't know what to discuss. You can also ask to review your goals, talk about what happened since your last session, or identify what has been bothering you most recently. Therapy doesn't require you to arrive with a perfectly organized agenda.

## 4. Should I tell my therapist about intrusive thoughts?

Yes, particularly if the thoughts are distressing, frequent, or causing compulsions, checking, avoidance, or reassurance-seeking. You can begin by saying that you have unwanted thoughts that frighten or embarrass you. Your therapist can ask appropriate follow-up questions and help determine what support may be useful.

## 5. Do I have to talk about my childhood in therapy?

Not necessarily. Your therapist may ask about your past if it appears relevant to your current concerns, but you don't have to tell your entire life story immediately. You can discuss what feels relevant and tell your therapist if you're not ready to discuss a particular experience.

## 6. What if I'm embarrassed to tell my therapist something?

You can start by telling the therapist that you're embarrassed. For example: “There's something I want to tell you, but I'm afraid you'll judge me.” This can make it easier to begin the conversation and lets your therapist understand why the topic feels difficult.

## 7. Can I talk about my relationship in individual therapy?

Yes. Relationships can have a major effect on mental health. You can discuss communication problems, conflict, trust, boundaries, breakups, attachment, loneliness, or other relationship concerns even when your partner isn't attending therapy.

## 8. Should I talk about things that happened between therapy sessions?

Absolutely. Events between sessions can provide important information about your progress and challenges. Tell your therapist about significant changes, difficult situations, setbacks, improvements, or moments when you used something you learned in therapy.

## 9. What if I disagree with my therapist?

You can discuss your disagreement. Therapy is generally collaborative, and it's reasonable to ask why your therapist recommends a particular approach. If you consistently feel misunderstood or believe the treatment isn't appropriate, discussing those concerns or considering another qualified professional may be appropriate.

## 10. What if I have nothing to talk about in therapy?

Having a quiet or uneventful week doesn't mean therapy has stopped being useful. You can review your goals, discuss patterns you've noticed, explore how therapy itself is going, or examine something that has been on your mind even if it seems minor. Your therapist can help create a direction for the session.

# Featured Snippet Answers

### What should I talk about in therapy?

Talk about what is bothering you, affecting your life, or something you want to understand or change. This might include emotions, thoughts, relationships, work, sleep, difficult experiences, habits, fears, or personal goals. You don't need to know exactly what's wrong before starting therapy. Your therapist can help guide the conversation.

### What should I say in my first therapy session?

Start by explaining why you decided to seek therapy, what you've been experiencing, how long it has been happening, how it affects your life, and what you hope will change. You can also simply say, “I'm not sure where to start.” Your therapist can ask questions to help organize the conversation.

### What if I don't know what to talk about in therapy?

Tell your therapist you don't know what to talk about. You can discuss what has been bothering you recently, what happened since your last session, your treatment goals, or how you feel therapy is going. You don't need to arrive with a prepared agenda.

### Do I have to tell my therapist everything?

No. You can decide what you're comfortable discussing and how quickly you disclose personal information. However, being honest about symptoms, behaviors, and safety concerns can help your therapist provide appropriate care. If you're not ready to discuss something, you can tell your therapist that.

# Final Takeaway

You don't need to walk into therapy knowing exactly what to say.

Start with the thing that made you seek help.

**“I've been struggling with…”**

**“I keep thinking about…”**

**“I don't understand why I…”**

**“I want to change…”**

Those are all valid starting points.

You can talk about your emotions, thoughts, relationships, habits, fears, sleep, work, difficult experiences, or anything else that seems relevant. And if you're completely unsure where to begin, **tell your therapist that**.

You don't have to organize your mind before asking someone to help you understand it.

That's one of the reasons therapy exists.
