# Can Anxiety Cause Insomnia? How Anxiety Affects Your Sleep

If you find yourself lying awake at night thinking about tomorrow, replaying conversations, or worrying about things that could go wrong, you may wonder, **“Can anxiety cause insomnia?”**

Yes. **Anxiety can contribute to insomnia and other sleep problems.** Worry, racing thoughts, physical tension, and increased mental alertness can make it harder to fall asleep or stay asleep. People with anxiety may also wake during the night and find it difficult to quiet their thoughts enough to return to sleep.

However, anxiety is only one possible cause of insomnia. Stress, medications, caffeine, alcohol, pain, sleep disorders, an irregular sleep schedule, environmental factors, and other physical or mental-health conditions can also affect sleep.

Anxiety and insomnia can also reinforce each other. Anxiety may interfere with sleep, while repeated poor sleep can make some people feel more anxious, irritable, or overwhelmed the following day.

## How Can Anxiety Cause Insomnia?

Sleep requires a shift from alertness toward relaxation. Anxiety can make that shift more difficult.

When you're anxious, your attention may stay focused on possible problems. Instead of naturally winding down, your mind may continue analyzing, planning, predicting, or checking for danger.

For example, you might think:

- “What if I make a mistake tomorrow?”
    
- “Did I handle that conversation badly?”
    
- “What if I can't sleep tonight?”
    
- “How will I get through tomorrow if I'm exhausted?”
    
- “What if something goes wrong?”
    

These thoughts can keep your brain mentally engaged when you are trying to sleep.

Anxiety can also involve physical tension and difficulty relaxing. The National Institute of Mental Health notes that anxiety symptoms can include feeling restless or on edge, difficulty relaxing, muscle tension, and trouble falling or staying asleep.

This can create a cycle:

**Anxiety → difficulty sleeping → fatigue → increased stress → more anxiety → more difficulty sleeping**

This cycle does not happen to everyone, and having occasional anxiety-related sleep difficulty does not mean you have an anxiety disorder.

## Why Does Anxiety Feel Worse at Night?

Many people notice that anxiety becomes more noticeable when the day becomes quiet.

During the daytime, work, school, conversations, errands, entertainment, and other activities can occupy your attention. At night, there may be fewer distractions.

That can give worries more room to surface.

You might start:

- replaying something that happened earlier
    
- worrying about tomorrow
    
- thinking about an unresolved problem
    
- imagining possible future situations
    
- paying close attention to your heartbeat or breathing
    
- becoming frustrated because you're still awake
    

This is sometimes described as **anxiety at night** or **overthinking at night**.

Nighttime anxiety does not automatically mean that you have an anxiety disorder. Everyone experiences worry from time to time. The important question is whether anxiety and sleep difficulties are persistent, distressing, or interfering with everyday life.

## Can Anxiety Cause Trouble Falling Asleep?

Yes. Anxiety can make the transition from wakefulness to sleep more difficult.

One reason is **mental arousal**. If your mind is actively solving problems or anticipating threats, it may be difficult to settle into a relaxed state.

Sleep anxiety can add another layer.

You may initially worry about something unrelated to sleep. Then you notice that you're still awake and begin worrying about sleep itself:

> “What if I can't sleep tonight?”

> “I have to wake up early.”

> “I'm going to be exhausted tomorrow.”

> “Why am I still awake?”

The more pressure you put on yourself to fall asleep immediately, the more frustrated and alert you may become.

This doesn't mean you are doing anything wrong. Sleep is not something that can always be forced.

## Can Anxiety Wake You Up During the Night?

Anxiety and stress can be associated with disrupted sleep, including waking during the night.

Someone experiencing anxiety-related nighttime awakenings might wake up and immediately notice:

- racing thoughts
    
- physical tension
    
- worry about tomorrow
    
- concern about a personal problem
    
- frustration about being awake
    
- repeated checking of the time
    

The difficulty may then become returning to sleep.

However, **not every nighttime awakening is caused by anxiety**. People naturally experience brief awakenings during sleep, and waking can also be related to noise, temperature, pain, bathroom needs, alcohol, medications, sleep apnea, and other factors.

## Can Anxiety Cause Early-Morning Awakening?

Early waking can occur for many different reasons.

Stress and anxiety may contribute to disrupted sleep for some people, but waking earlier than desired is **not proof of anxiety or depression**.

Sleep timing can be affected by your sleep schedule, environment, age, stress levels, medications, other health conditions, and sleep disorders.

If early-morning awakening happens repeatedly and leaves you tired or affects your daytime functioning, consider discussing it with a healthcare professional.

## What Does Anxiety-Related Insomnia Feel Like?

People experience sleep difficulties differently, but anxiety may be involved when you notice patterns such as:

- feeling physically tired but mentally alert
    
- lying awake while thinking about problems
    
- experiencing racing thoughts at night
    
- replaying conversations or events
    
- imagining things that might go wrong
    
- feeling tense when you get into bed
    
- waking and immediately becoming worried
    
- becoming frustrated about not sleeping
    
- worrying about how tired you'll feel the next day
    

These experiences can happen with ordinary stress as well as with mental-health conditions. They **cannot be used by themselves to diagnose an anxiety disorder or insomnia**.

## Anxiety vs. Insomnia: What's the Difference?

Anxiety and insomnia overlap, but they are not the same thing.

|Anxiety|Insomnia|
|---|---|
|Excessive worry or fear may be prominent|Difficulty sleeping is the main concern|
|Can involve physical and mental symptoms|Can involve difficulty falling or staying asleep|
|May occur during the day and at night|Primarily involves sleep and its daytime consequences|
|Can contribute to sleep problems|Poor sleep can contribute to emotional difficulties|

A person can experience anxiety without insomnia. Someone can also experience insomnia without significant anxiety.

Some people experience both.

## Can Lack of Sleep Make Anxiety Worse?

The relationship between anxiety and sleep can work in both directions.

Anxiety may make sleep more difficult, while insufficient or disrupted sleep may make emotional regulation and coping more difficult the next day.

Poor sleep can contribute to tiredness, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and reduced ability to handle everyday stress. MedlinePlus also notes that sleep deprivation can affect mood and may contribute to anxiety.

This does not mean that one bad night will automatically make someone anxious. Occasional poor sleep is common. The concern is more about persistent or repeated sleep disruption.

## How Can I Sleep When Anxiety Is Keeping Me Awake?

If anxiety is making it difficult to sleep, the goal is not to force yourself to fall asleep. Instead, focus on reducing stimulation and giving your mind and body an opportunity to settle.

### 1. Create a wind-down period

Give yourself some time between demanding daytime activities and going to bed.

During this period, consider:

- finishing work where possible
    
- lowering the intensity of activities
    
- preparing things you need for tomorrow
    
- taking a shower or bath
    
- reading something relaxing
    
- listening to calm audio
    
- following a predictable bedtime routine
    

A consistent wind-down routine can provide a clear transition into sleep.

### 2. Write down your worries

If your thoughts keep returning to the same problems, write them down before bed.

You can divide the page into three sections:

**What is worrying me?**

Write the concern honestly.

**What can I control?**

Separate the things you can influence from things you cannot control tonight.

**What can I do tomorrow?**

Choose one realistic next step.

The purpose isn't to solve every problem before bed. It is to give your mind somewhere to put unfinished thoughts.

### 3. Try slow, comfortable breathing

Slow breathing may help some people shift attention away from anxious thoughts.

Try this:

1. Breathe in gently through your nose.
    
2. Let the breath out slowly and comfortably.
    
3. Avoid forcing the breath or holding it for long periods.
    
4. Repeat for several breaths.
    
5. If focusing on breathing makes you more anxious, return your attention to another calming activity.
    

The goal is comfort, not achieving a particular breathing score or forcing yourself to relax.

### 4. Use grounding

When racing thoughts are taking over, gently bring your attention toward the present moment.

For example, notice:

- five things you can see
    
- four things you can physically feel
    
- three sounds you can hear
    
- two things you can smell
    
- one thing you can taste or imagine tasting
    

You don't need to perform the exercise perfectly. The idea is simply to shift attention away from future worries and toward present sensations.

### 5. Reduce stimulating activities before bed

If anxiety is affecting your sleep, consider reducing activities that leave you highly alert close to bedtime.

These might include:

- intense work
    
- emotionally upsetting content
    
- highly competitive gaming
    
- stressful conversations
    
- repeatedly checking messages
    
- other activities that keep your mind highly engaged
    

Phone or computer use is not automatically harmful for everyone, but the content and level of stimulation can make winding down harder for some people.

### 6. Make your bedroom comfortable

Your sleep environment can also influence sleep.

Consider:

- keeping the room comfortably cool
    
- reducing unnecessary noise
    
- making the room appropriately dark
    
- using comfortable bedding
    
- keeping the bedroom associated mainly with sleep and relaxation
    

### 7. Avoid repeatedly checking the clock

Looking at the clock again and again can turn sleep into a countdown.

You might think:

“It's 1:00.”

Then:

“Now it's 2:00.”

Then:

“I only have five hours left.”

That pattern can increase frustration and worry.

If clock-watching makes you more anxious, consider placing the clock where you cannot easily see it.

### 8. Don't stay in bed increasingly frustrated

If you're awake and becoming increasingly frustrated, continuing to struggle with sleep in bed may reinforce the association between your bed and wakefulness.

Stimulus-control approaches used in CBT-I generally encourage getting out of bed when you cannot sleep and returning when you feel sleepy, while avoiding stimulating activities.

A quiet activity in low light may be preferable. The goal is not to punish yourself or follow a rigid rule; it is to reduce the cycle of frustration and wakefulness in bed.

## How to Calm Anxiety Before Bed

A simple evening routine might look like this:

**1–2 hours before bed**

- reduce demanding or stimulating activities
    
- finish important tasks where possible
    
- prepare clothes or items for tomorrow
    
- write down unresolved worries
    
- move into a quieter routine
    

**At bedtime**

- keep the room comfortable
    
- practice slow, comfortable breathing if helpful
    
- relax your body
    
- focus attention on present sensations
    
- avoid trying to force sleep
    

You may not fall asleep immediately, and that's okay. The purpose of a routine is to create conditions that support sleep, not guarantee it on demand.

## What Should I Avoid If Anxiety Is Affecting My Sleep?

Some habits can interfere with sleep or make nighttime anxiety harder to manage.

Depending on the individual, it may help to avoid:

- excessive caffeine, particularly later in the day
    
- using alcohol as a sleep aid
    
- recreational substances as a way to manage sleep
    
- repeatedly checking the clock
    
- spending long periods worrying in bed
    
- working in bed
    
- highly stimulating activities immediately before bedtime
    
- long or poorly timed naps if they interfere with nighttime sleep
    

Caffeine, alcohol, irregular schedules, some medications, and other substances can affect sleep.

## How Can I Improve Sleep Long Term If I Have Anxiety?

Short-term calming strategies can be useful, but long-term sleep often benefits from consistent habits.

Consider:

- keeping a relatively consistent wake time
    
- getting regular physical activity
    
- getting appropriate daytime light exposure
    
- maintaining a predictable bedtime routine
    
- addressing worries during the daytime rather than only at bedtime
    
- limiting caffeine when it interferes with sleep
    
- keeping your sleep environment comfortable
    
- avoiding naps that make it harder to sleep at night
    

These habits can support healthy sleep, but they are not guaranteed to resolve persistent insomnia.

If sleep problems continue, it is worth looking beyond sleep habits alone. Insomnia can have behavioral, psychological, environmental, medication-related, medical, and sleep-related causes.

## Can Therapy Help With Anxiety and Insomnia?

Yes. Professional treatment may be helpful when anxiety or sleep problems are persistent or interfering with daily life.

**Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** can help people identify and change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior associated with anxiety. CBT can also be used for sleep problems.

For persistent insomnia, **cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** is a structured treatment specifically designed to address factors that maintain insomnia. The American College of Physicians recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for adults with chronic insomnia.

Treatment may focus on sleep habits, thoughts and beliefs about sleep, stimulus control, and other behavioral approaches.

If anxiety is contributing to sleep problems, a healthcare professional can also help determine whether the anxiety itself needs attention.

## What If I'm Afraid I Won't Sleep?

Fear about sleep can become part of the problem.

The cycle may look like this:

**Worry about sleep → increased alertness → difficulty sleeping → more worry about sleep**

For example, after one poor night, you may begin thinking:

> “I have to sleep tonight.”

That pressure can make bedtime feel like a test you must pass.

Instead, try shifting the goal from **forcing sleep** to **creating the conditions for sleep**.

You can:

- avoid repeatedly checking the time
    
- follow a consistent routine
    
- write worries down before bed
    
- use a quiet activity if you're awake and frustrated
    
- remind yourself that one poor night does not determine how the next day will go
    
- address ongoing worries during the daytime
    

You do not need to stop every thought before you can sleep. Trying to completely empty your mind can sometimes create even more pressure.

## How Much Sleep Do Adults Need?

Sleep needs vary between individuals, but the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that healthy adults generally obtain **7 or more hours of sleep per night on a regular basis** to support health and daytime functioning. Individual sleep needs can vary based on factors such as age, health, behavior, and other circumstances.

The quality and regularity of sleep matter too. A person who occasionally sleeps less than usual does not necessarily have a sleep disorder.

## When Does Trouble Sleeping Become Insomnia?

Insomnia generally involves difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking earlier than desired, with resulting problems such as daytime tiredness or difficulty functioning.

Short-term insomnia can occur during periods of stress and may last days or weeks. Chronic insomnia is more persistent and can be associated with other medical, psychological, behavioral, or sleep-related factors.

A few nights of poor sleep do not automatically mean you have insomnia.

If sleep difficulties continue, recur frequently, or significantly affect your daytime life, a healthcare professional can help determine what may be contributing to them.

## When Should I See a Doctor or Sleep Professional?

Consider seeking professional advice if:

- sleep problems persist
    
- you regularly feel exhausted during the day
    
- difficulty sleeping affects work, school, or relationships
    
- you have repeated nighttime awakenings
    
- anxiety is persistent or difficult to manage
    
- sleep problems are affecting your quality of life
    
- symptoms are becoming worse
    
- you snore loudly or experience breathing interruptions during sleep
    
- you notice unusual movements or behaviors during sleep
    

There can be many causes of sleep problems, including anxiety, depression, pain, medications, caffeine or alcohol, sleep apnea, irregular schedules, and other medical or sleep conditions.

A professional evaluation can help identify the underlying factors instead of assuming that anxiety is the cause.

## When Should I Seek Urgent Help?

Seek urgent professional help if severe mental-health symptoms are making it difficult to function safely, you become severely confused, or you feel that you may hurt yourself or someone else.

If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or believe you are in immediate danger, contact your **local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service** right away.

You do not have to manage a mental-health crisis alone.

# Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Can anxiety cause insomnia?

Yes. Anxiety can contribute to insomnia by increasing worry, mental alertness, and physical tension. It may make falling asleep, staying asleep, or returning to sleep more difficult. However, insomnia has many possible causes.

### 2. Why does anxiety make it hard to sleep?

Anxiety can keep your attention focused on worries and possible problems. Racing thoughts, physical tension, and difficulty relaxing may make it harder to transition from wakefulness into sleep.

### 3. Can anxiety keep you awake all night?

It can contribute to prolonged wakefulness, particularly when worry and frustration keep increasing. However, being awake all night is not necessarily caused by anxiety, and other sleep or health factors may be involved.

### 4. How can I sleep when anxiety is keeping me awake?

Try creating a quiet wind-down routine, writing down worries, using comfortable slow breathing, avoiding clock-watching, and stepping out of bed for a quiet activity if you become increasingly frustrated while awake.

### 5. Can anxiety cause nighttime awakenings?

Anxiety and stress can be associated with disrupted sleep and nighttime awakenings. But waking during the night can have many other causes, including environmental factors, pain, medications, alcohol, and sleep disorders.

### 6. Why does my anxiety get worse at night?

Nighttime often has fewer distractions, so worries may become more noticeable. You may also start thinking about the day, tomorrow's responsibilities, unresolved problems, or the consequences of not sleeping.

### 7. Can lack of sleep make anxiety worse?

Poor or insufficient sleep can affect mood, concentration, and the ability to cope with stress. For some people, this can make anxiety feel harder to manage.

### 8. How do I calm anxiety before bed?

Try reducing stimulating activities, preparing for the next day, writing down worries, practicing comfortable slow breathing, and using a predictable wind-down routine. These strategies may support relaxation but cannot guarantee immediate sleep.

### 9. Can therapy help anxiety-related insomnia?

Yes. CBT can help with anxiety and sleep problems, while CBT-I is a structured treatment specifically designed for chronic insomnia.

### 10. When should I see a doctor about insomnia?

Consider speaking with a healthcare professional when sleep problems persist, interfere with daytime functioning or quality of life, occur alongside persistent anxiety, or are accompanied by symptoms such as loud snoring or breathing interruptions.

# The Bottom Line

**Can anxiety cause insomnia? Yes, it can.**

Anxiety may make it harder to sleep by keeping your mind alert, increasing worry, creating physical tension, and making it difficult to relax. Nighttime can make these thoughts more noticeable because there are fewer distractions.

At the same time, **not every case of insomnia is caused by anxiety**. Sleep difficulties can have many causes, and poor sleep can sometimes make anxiety and stress more difficult to manage.

If anxiety is keeping you awake occasionally, simple changes to your evening routine may help. If sleep problems are persistent, worsening, or affecting your ability to function during the day, consider talking with a healthcare professional. Effective treatments are available, including CBT and CBT-I when appropriate.
