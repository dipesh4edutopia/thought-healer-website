# How to Get Out of Depression: Steps That Can Help You Feel Better

If you are struggling with depression, you may be wondering, **"How do I get out of depression?"** There is no single solution that works for everyone, and depression is not something you can always overcome through willpower alone. But depression is treatable, and several forms of support can help. Treatment may include psychotherapy, medication prescribed by a healthcare professional, or a combination of approaches. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

Alongside professional care, small steps such as maintaining a routine, staying connected with supportive people, getting regular movement, protecting your sleep, and breaking tasks into manageable pieces may help you cope with symptoms.

If depression is severe, persistent, worsening, or affecting your ability to function, professional support is important. And if you are thinking about suicide or self-harm, seek immediate help rather than trying to manage it alone.

# How Do You Get Out of Depression?

There is no quick fix for depression.

For some people, symptoms improve with psychotherapy. Others benefit from medication, or a combination of therapy and medication. Lifestyle changes and social support can also play an important supportive role. The right approach depends on the person, their symptoms, their circumstances, and the type and severity of depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

The most important first step is often **getting support rather than trying to handle everything alone**.

If you are depressed, you may have very little energy or motivation. That means advice like "just exercise more" or "think positively" can feel impossible.

A better approach is to start **very small**.

---

# What Does "Getting Out of Depression" Actually Mean?

Recovery does not necessarily mean waking up one morning and suddenly feeling happy.

It may look more like:

- Getting out of bed a little more easily
    
- Having enough energy to shower
    
- Answering a message from a friend
    
- Sleeping more regularly
    
- Feeling interested in something again
    
- Returning to work or school gradually
    
- Having fewer hopeless thoughts
    
- Being able to complete everyday tasks
    
- Experiencing moments of enjoyment
    
- Feeling more connected to other people
    

Recovery can be gradual.

Some days may feel better than others.

A difficult day does not necessarily mean you are going backward.

# 1. Talk to a Mental-Health Professional

One of the most important steps you can take is to seek professional support.

You can start with:

- A primary-care doctor
    
- Psychologist
    
- Psychiatrist
    
- Qualified therapist
    

A professional can assess your symptoms and determine whether depression may be involved.

They can also consider other factors that can produce similar symptoms, including physical health conditions, medications, substance use, sleep problems, anxiety, grief, or other mental-health conditions.

You do not need to diagnose yourself before making an appointment.

You can simply say:

> "I've been feeling low for a while, I've lost interest in things, and I'm struggling to function normally."

That is enough information to begin the conversation.

---

# 2. Consider Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy, sometimes called talk therapy, is an important treatment for depression.

Different forms of therapy may be used depending on the person's needs.

Therapy can help you:

- Understand patterns affecting your mood
    
- Identify unhelpful thinking patterns
    
- Develop coping skills
    
- Work through difficult experiences
    
- Improve relationships
    
- Build healthier routines
    
- Address behaviors that maintain low mood
    

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), for example, is one evidence-based approach used to treat depression. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

Therapy is not simply "talking about your problems."

A qualified therapist can help you develop practical strategies for managing symptoms and functioning more effectively.

---

# 3. Ask a Doctor About Treatment Options

Depending on your symptoms and circumstances, a healthcare professional may discuss different treatment options.

Treatment can include:

- Psychotherapy
    
- Medication
    
- Psychotherapy combined with medication
    
- Other treatments for certain forms or levels of depression
    

Medication decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare professional.

Do not start, stop, increase, or reduce prescription medication on your own.

If you are already taking medication and are concerned about side effects or effectiveness, discuss this with the prescribing professional rather than changing the dose yourself. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

# 4. Start With One Small Task

Depression can make ordinary tasks feel enormous.

You may know exactly what needs to be done but feel unable to start.

Instead of asking:

**"How am I going to fix my whole life?"**

ask:

**"What is the smallest useful thing I can do right now?"**

That might be:

- Getting out of bed
    
- Drinking some water
    
- Taking a shower
    
- Opening the curtains
    
- Eating something
    
- Answering one message
    
- Walking outside for five minutes
    
- Washing a few dishes
    

You do not have to complete everything.

One small action is still progress.

---

# 5. Create a Simple Daily Routine

Depression can disrupt your sense of structure.

You may sleep at unpredictable times, skip meals, stay in bed for long periods, or lose track of the day.

A simple routine can provide some stability.

Try to establish consistent times for:

- Waking up
    
- Eating meals
    
- Taking medication if prescribed
    
- Work or study
    
- Physical activity
    
- Relaxation
    
- Going to bed
    

Do not create an elaborate schedule that becomes another source of pressure.

A few consistent habits are enough to begin.

---

# 6. Get Some Physical Activity

Physical activity can be part of supporting mental wellbeing.

You do not have to start with a gym membership or intense workout.

Try something manageable:

- A short walk
    
- Gentle stretching
    
- Cycling
    
- Dancing at home
    
- Gardening
    
- Another activity you can realistically maintain
    

The goal is not to "exercise your depression away."

Depression is a health condition, and exercise is not a substitute for appropriate treatment.

Instead, think of movement as **one supportive part of recovery**.

---

# 7. Take Your Sleep Seriously

Depression can affect sleep, and poor sleep can make emotional symptoms harder to manage.

Try to:

- Keep a reasonably consistent sleep schedule
    
- Wake around the same time each day
    
- Create a calming bedtime routine
    
- Reduce activities that keep you mentally stimulated immediately before bed
    
- Make your sleeping environment comfortable
    

If you are sleeping far too much, barely sleeping, or experiencing persistent sleep problems, discuss this with a healthcare professional.

Sleep changes can be a symptom of depression and can also have other causes. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

# 8. Stay Connected With People

Depression often encourages isolation.

You may think:

**"I don't want to bother anyone."**

Or:

**"Nobody will understand."**

But withdrawing completely can make depression harder to manage.

You do not need to have a long conversation.

Try:

- Sending a text
    
- Calling someone
    
- Sitting with a family member
    
- Going for a walk with a friend
    
- Having a meal with someone
    
- Asking someone to check in on you
    

You can even tell someone:

> "I'm going through a difficult period. I don't need you to fix it—I just don't want to be alone with it."

---

# 9. Do Not Wait Until You Feel Motivated

This can be one of the hardest parts of depression.

Depression can reduce motivation.

You may think:

**"I'll start when I feel better."**

But sometimes feeling better comes **after** taking a small action rather than before it.

For example:

**Get dressed → leave the house → take a short walk → return home**

You may not feel enthusiastic beforehand.

That is okay.

The goal is not to force happiness. It is to gradually rebuild activity and connection.

---

# 10. Do Things You Used to Enjoy—Without Expecting Immediate Pleasure

If depression has caused you to lose interest in everything, your favorite activities may not feel enjoyable right now.

That does not necessarily mean you will never enjoy them again.

Try returning to activities in small doses:

- Listen to music
    
- Watch a favorite show
    
- Cook something familiar
    
- Spend time outdoors
    
- Play a game
    
- Read
    
- Spend time with someone you trust
    

Do not judge the activity by whether it immediately makes you happy.

Sometimes the first goal is simply **reconnecting with the activity**.

---

# 11. Challenge the "Nothing Will Help" Thought

Depression can make hopeless thoughts feel like facts.

You might think:

- "Nothing will change."
    
- "I've tried everything."
    
- "I'm always going to feel this way."
    
- "I'm a failure."
    
- "There is no point."
    

Try creating some distance from the thought.

Instead of:

**"Nothing will ever get better."**

try:

**"Right now, I can't imagine things getting better."**

Those statements are different.

The second recognizes how you feel without treating that feeling as a prediction of the future.

If hopeless thoughts are persistent or severe, professional support is particularly important.

---

# 12. Eat Regularly and Look After Your Basic Needs

Depression can make basic self-care surprisingly difficult.

You may forget to eat, have little appetite, or lack the energy to prepare food.

Try to make meals as simple as necessary.

You do not need a perfect diet.

Focus on maintaining basic nutrition and hydration.

If you have significant changes in appetite or weight, discuss them with a healthcare professional because they can occur with depression but can also have other causes. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

---

# 13. Reduce Alcohol and Avoid Recreational Drugs as a Coping Strategy

When you feel emotionally overwhelmed, it can be tempting to use alcohol or recreational drugs to numb your feelings.

This may provide temporary relief but can make mood, sleep, anxiety, and other problems worse.

If substance use has become part of how you cope with depression, consider discussing it honestly with a healthcare professional.

You deserve support without judgment.

---

# 14. Spend Time Outside When You Can

When depression is severe, leaving the house can feel like a major task.

If possible, start small.

Open a window.

Sit outside for a few minutes.

Stand on a balcony.

Walk around the block.

The goal is not to cure depression through sunlight or fresh air.

It is simply to introduce small amounts of movement, environmental change, and routine into your day.

---

# 15. Track Your Symptoms and Progress

Depression can make improvement difficult to notice.

You may feel like:

**"Nothing has changed."**

Keeping a simple record can sometimes reveal small improvements.

You could track:

- Mood
    
- Sleep
    
- Energy
    
- Appetite
    
- Social contact
    
- Physical activity
    
- Enjoyment
    
- Important tasks completed
    

You do not need to record everything.

A few notes each day may be enough.

This information can also be useful during appointments with your healthcare professional.

# Can You Get Out of Depression Without Treatment?

Some periods of low mood improve with time, especially when they are connected to temporary stress or difficult circumstances.

But significant depression should not simply be left untreated.

Depression is a treatable health condition, and professional treatment can help many people. ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression))

If symptoms are persistent, severe, worsening, or interfering with everyday life, seeking professional support is a better approach than waiting indefinitely for them to disappear.

And seeking treatment does not mean you have failed to cope on your own.

---

# How Long Does It Take to Recover From Depression?

There is no universal recovery timeline.

It depends on factors such as:

- The type of depression
    
- Severity of symptoms
    
- How long symptoms have been present
    
- Other health conditions
    
- Stress and life circumstances
    
- Treatment
    
- Individual response to treatment
    
- Available social support
    

Some people notice improvement gradually, while others may need adjustments to their treatment and longer-term support.

Recovery is not always linear.

You may have good days and difficult days.

A difficult day does not erase progress.

---

# What If I Keep Relapsing?

Depression can return for some people.

If symptoms come back, it does not mean previous treatment was pointless or that you have failed.

It may mean you need additional support or a change in your treatment plan.

If you notice familiar symptoms returning—such as persistent low mood, loss of interest, sleep changes, withdrawal, or hopelessness—consider contacting your healthcare professional early.

Early support may make it easier to address worsening symptoms.

---

# What If I Have No Motivation to Get Help?

This is common.

Depression itself can make seeking help feel impossible.

You can make the first step extremely small.

Instead of:

**"I need to find a therapist and completely fix this."**

try:

**"I'll tell one person that I'm struggling."**

Then:

**"I'll make one appointment."**

You can ask someone you trust to help you find a professional or accompany you to an appointment if appropriate.

You do not need to feel motivated before asking for help.

---

# When Should I Get Professional Help for Depression?

Consider seeking professional support if:

- You feel persistently sad or empty
    
- You have stopped enjoying things
    
- Symptoms have lasted around two weeks or longer
    
- You feel hopeless or worthless
    
- Your energy is consistently low
    
- Your sleep has changed significantly
    
- Your appetite has changed
    
- You are struggling to concentrate
    
- You are withdrawing from others
    
- Work, school, or relationships are being affected
    
- Everyday tasks are becoming difficult
    
- You are unsure whether you may be depressed
    

A primary-care doctor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or qualified therapist can help you understand what you are experiencing.

You do not need to wait until depression becomes severe.

---

# When Should I Seek Emergency Help?

Depression can sometimes involve suicidal thoughts or thoughts of self-harm.

Take these thoughts seriously.

Seek immediate help if you:

- Are thinking about killing yourself
    
- Are thinking about seriously hurting yourself
    
- Have made a suicide plan
    
- Have recently attempted suicide
    
- Feel unable to keep yourself safe
    

If you are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency service or go to the nearest emergency department.

If you are supporting someone who may be at immediate risk, stay with them where possible and help them connect with urgent professional support.

# The Bottom Line

If you are asking **"How do I get out of depression?"**, remember that you do not have to solve everything at once.

Depression is a treatable health condition, and recovery can involve professional treatment, practical daily support, social connection, and time.

Start with the smallest realistic step.

Talk to someone.

Make an appointment.

Take a shower.

Eat something.

Go outside for a few minutes.

Take a short walk.

Complete one manageable task.

You do not need to feel hopeful before taking the first step.

And if you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or feel unable to keep yourself safe, seek immediate help rather than trying to get through it alone.
