# How to Help Someone With Depression: What to Say and Do

**How do you help someone with depression?** Start by listening without judgment, staying connected, offering practical help, and encouraging professional support when appropriate. You do not have to fix someone's depression to support them. Small actions—checking in, helping with everyday tasks, or simply being present—can help reduce isolation and make it easier for someone to seek care.

Depression is more than simply having a bad day or feeling sad. It can affect a person's energy, motivation, sleep, appetite, concentration, relationships, work, and ability to manage everyday responsibilities. Supporting someone can be difficult, particularly when they withdraw or seem unable to accept help. The goal is not to take control of their life, but to let them know they do not have to face everything alone.

> **Important:** You cannot diagnose depression based on someone's behavior. Only a qualified healthcare or mental-health professional can make a diagnosis.

## What Is Depression?

Depression is a mental-health condition that can involve persistent changes in mood, interest, energy, thinking, behavior, and everyday functioning.

Someone experiencing depression may have changes in:

- Mood, including sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, or irritability
    
- Interest or enjoyment in activities they normally like
    
- Energy and motivation
    
- Sleep
    
- Appetite or eating patterns
    
- Concentration and decision-making
    
- Self-esteem or feelings about themselves
    
- Thoughts about the future
    
- Ability to manage work, school, relationships, or daily responsibilities
    

Not everyone experiences depression in the same way, and not everyone has every symptom. Some symptoms can also be caused by medical conditions, medications, stress, grief, or other mental-health concerns. A healthcare professional can assess what may be happening and determine whether treatment is appropriate.

## How Can I Tell If Someone May Be Struggling With Depression?

There is no single behavior that proves someone has depression. However, you may notice a pattern of changes that concerns you.

Possible signs someone may be struggling include:

- Spending less time with friends or family
    
- Losing interest in hobbies or activities they once enjoyed
    
- Appearing persistently sad, empty, or low
    
- Becoming unusually irritable or frustrated
    
- Having very little energy
    
- Sleeping much more or much less than usual
    
- Eating significantly more or less than usual
    
- Finding it difficult to concentrate
    
- Struggling with work, school, household tasks, or personal responsibilities
    
- Neglecting personal care or their living environment
    
- Expressing hopelessness, worthlessness, or feeling like a burden
    
- Talking about death, suicide, self-harm, or not wanting to be alive
    

These changes can have many different causes. Think of them as reasons to **check in**, not as a way to diagnose someone.

## How Do I Approach Someone With Depression?

Starting a conversation can feel uncomfortable. You may worry about saying the wrong thing. You do not need the perfect words. Genuine concern and a willingness to listen are often more important.

### 1. Choose an appropriate time and place

If possible, choose a private, relatively calm setting where you will not be interrupted.

You do not necessarily need to arrange a formal conversation. Sometimes talking while walking, having coffee, driving, or doing another ordinary activity can make the conversation feel less intimidating.

### 2. Start with an observation

Instead of telling the person what is wrong, describe what you have noticed.

You might say:

> “I've noticed you haven't seemed like yourself lately.”

Or:

> “I've noticed you've been having a difficult time. I'm here if you want to talk.”

This gives the person an opportunity to explain what they are experiencing without feeling labeled.

### 3. Ask open-ended questions

Questions that invite conversation are generally more helpful than questions that can be answered with a simple yes or no.

Try:

- “How have you really been feeling lately?”
    
- “What has been hardest for you?”
    
- “How have things been going day to day?”
    
- “What would make things a little easier right now?”
    
- “How can I support you?”
    

### 4. Listen rather than immediately solving

When someone is struggling, it can be tempting to immediately offer solutions.

Sometimes what they need first is to feel heard.

You can listen, acknowledge what they are saying, and ask whether they want advice before offering it.

For example:

> “That sounds really difficult. Do you want me to just listen, or would you like us to think about what might help?”

### 5. Avoid judgment

Depression is not simply a lack of willpower. A person may genuinely want to do something but feel unable to get started.

Patience matters. NHS guidance recommends listening, accepting someone without judgment, staying in contact, offering practical help, and encouraging appropriate professional support.

## What Should I Say to Someone With Depression?

There is no single perfect sentence. The most helpful words usually communicate **care, patience, and acceptance**.

You could say:

- “I'm here for you.”
    
- “You don't have to go through this alone.”
    
- “I'm glad you told me.”
    
- “I may not fully understand what you're experiencing, but I want to support you.”
    
- “You don't have to explain everything right now.”
    
- “Would it help if I stayed with you?”
    
- “What would feel helpful today?”
    
- “Can I help you find professional support?”
    
- “We can take this one step at a time.”
    

### Featured-snippet answer: What should I say to someone with depression?

**Tell them you care, listen without judging, acknowledge that what they're experiencing is difficult, and let them know they do not have to handle it alone. Ask what kind of support would be helpful rather than assuming you know what they need.**

You do not have to say something profound. A simple, sincere message can be enough to keep a connection open.

---

## What Should I NOT Say to Someone With Depression?

Even well-intentioned comments can make someone feel misunderstood or blamed.

Try to avoid statements such as:

- “Just think positive.”
    
- “Everyone feels like this sometimes.”
    
- “You have nothing to be depressed about.”
    
- “You just need to get out more.”
    
- “Other people have it worse.”
    
- “You need to try harder.”
    
- “It's all in your head.”
    
- “Why can't you just snap out of it?”
    

These statements can minimize what the person is experiencing or make depression sound like a choice.

If you have said something like this before, that does not mean you cannot repair the conversation. You can simply acknowledge it:

> “I realize I may have tried to make things sound easier than they are. I'm sorry. I want to understand what you're going through.”

Then listen.

## How Can I Help Someone With Depression Day to Day?

Depression can make ordinary tasks feel overwhelming. Practical support can therefore be valuable.

Depending on what the person wants, you might offer to help with:

- Preparing or bringing a meal
    
- Grocery shopping
    
- Household chores
    
- Running errands
    
- Childcare
    
- Transportation
    
- Getting to an appointment
    
- Organizing paperwork
    
- Maintaining a simple daily routine
    
- Going for a short walk
    
- Spending quiet time together
    

Instead of saying:

> “Let me know if you need anything.”

try something more specific:

> “I'm going to the grocery store this evening. Would you like me to bring you something?”

Or:

> “I have time tomorrow. Would it help if I came over and helped with the laundry?”

Specific offers can be easier to accept than broad ones.

### Offer support without taking over

Helping someone does not mean taking complete control of their life.

Whenever possible, involve the person in decisions:

> “Would you like me to make the appointment, or would you rather do it yourself?”

The aim is to **support their autonomy**, not replace it.

---

## Should I Encourage Someone With Depression to See a Therapist?

Yes, encouraging professional support can be appropriate, especially when symptoms persist, become severe, or interfere with everyday life.

But encouragement works better when it does not feel like pressure.

You could say:

> “Would you be open to talking to a therapist or doctor?”

Or:

> “I can help you look at some options if you'd like.”

Other possibilities include:

- Helping them find a qualified professional
    
- Helping with transportation
    
- Sitting with them while they make a call
    
- Helping them prepare questions for an appointment
    
- Offering to accompany them if they want company
    

### Featured-snippet answer: How can I encourage someone with depression to get help?

**Encourage professional help gently by explaining that support is available, asking whether they would be open to it, and offering practical assistance such as finding a provider or attending an appointment with them. Avoid forcing or repeatedly pressuring them.**

Professional treatment for depression may include psychotherapy, medication prescribed by an appropriate healthcare professional, or a combination of approaches. Treatment should be tailored to the individual's needs and medical circumstances.

---

## What If They Don't Want Help?

Someone may refuse professional help for many reasons. Depression itself can make motivation and decision-making difficult. A person may also feel ashamed, frightened, hopeless, uncertain about treatment, or discouraged by a previous experience.

If they say no, you can:

- Keep the conversation open
    
- Continue checking in
    
- Offer small, specific forms of practical support
    
- Ask what is making help difficult
    
- Respect their choices when there is no immediate safety concern
    
- Offer to revisit the subject later
    
- Avoid turning every conversation into a discussion about treatment
    

For example:

> “I understand you're not ready right now. I'm still here, and we can talk about it again whenever you want.”

Refusing help today does not necessarily mean refusing help forever.

At the same time, **respecting autonomy does not mean ignoring a serious safety concern**. If you believe someone is in immediate danger of harming themselves, urgent professional or emergency support is appropriate.

---

## How Do I Help Someone With Depression Who Is Isolating Themselves?

Withdrawal can occur when someone is struggling with depression. They may not have the energy to socialize or may feel that they are a burden.

Try low-pressure connection rather than demanding that they “get out more.”

You could:

- Send a simple message
    
- Make a short phone call
    
- Sit with them quietly
    
- Offer to bring food
    
- Invite them on a short walk
    
- Watch a movie together
    
- Visit without expecting them to entertain you
    
- Check in even if they do not respond immediately
    

For example:

> “No need to reply. I just wanted you to know I'm thinking about you.”

Regular, gentle contact can help someone feel less alone. NHS guidance similarly recommends staying in touch through messages, phone calls, or meeting in person because depression can contribute to isolation.

---

## How Do I Help Someone With Depression Who Has No Motivation?

Depression can affect energy and motivation, so large tasks may feel impossible.

Try making the task smaller.

Instead of:

> “You need to clean your room.”

try:

> “Would you like me to help you clear one small area?”

Instead of:

> “You need to exercise.”

try:

> “Would you like to walk with me for ten minutes?”

The goal is not to force productivity. It is to reduce the size of the next step.

Small, manageable goals and practical support may be easier to approach than a long list of things the person “should” be doing.

---

## How Can I Support Someone With Depression From Far Away?

You can still provide meaningful support even when you do not live nearby.

Consider:

- Sending regular messages
    
- Scheduling occasional phone or video calls
    
- Asking how they are doing rather than only asking whether they completed tasks
    
- Helping them research professional support
    
- Helping arrange appointments if they want assistance
    
- Sending a meal or groceries where appropriate
    
- Coordinating with a trusted local person—with the person's knowledge when possible
    
- Maintaining consistent contact
    

Avoid turning support into constant surveillance.

A simple, predictable message can sometimes be more helpful than dozens of questions:

> “Thinking of you. I'm free to talk tonight if you'd like.”

---

## What If Someone With Depression Is Angry or Irritable?

Depression does not always look like sadness. Some people may become more irritable, frustrated, impatient, or emotionally reactive.

If this happens:

- Try not to interpret every reaction as a personal rejection
    
- Listen when the person is able to talk calmly
    
- Give them space when appropriate
    
- Set clear, respectful boundaries
    
- Avoid escalating arguments
    
- Step away if the situation becomes unsafe
    

Depression may help explain difficult behavior, but it does **not excuse abuse, threats, intimidation, or violence**.

You are allowed to protect yourself.

---

## What If I'm Worried They Might Hurt Themselves?

This is one situation where it is important to take your concerns seriously.

Warning signs may include talking about:

- Wanting to die
    
- Not wanting to live
    
- Suicide
    
- Self-harm
    
- Feeling hopeless
    
- Feeling like other people would be better off without them
    

If you are concerned, it is appropriate to ask directly about suicide. Asking about suicide does **not** cause someone to become suicidal. Direct, calm questions can help you understand whether there is an immediate safety concern.

You might ask:

> “Are you thinking about suicide?”

or:

> “Are you thinking about hurting yourself?”

If the person says yes, listen calmly and take the answer seriously.

If immediate danger is present:

- Stay with the person if it is safe for you to do so
    
- Encourage immediate professional or emergency assistance
    
- Contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service
    
- If it can be done safely, reduce access to immediate means of harm
    
- Do not promise to keep suicidal thoughts or plans secret
    
- Involve another trusted person or professional when necessary
    

For international readers, use the **official emergency or crisis resources for your country** rather than relying on a crisis number from another country. Official guidance from NIMH and MedlinePlus recommends seeking immediate help when someone is at imminent risk and not leaving them alone during an immediate life-threatening situation.

### Featured-snippet answer: What should I do if someone with depression talks about suicide?

**Take the statement seriously, stay calm, ask directly about immediate safety, and encourage urgent professional or emergency support. If there is imminent danger, stay with the person if it is safe and contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service. Do not promise to keep suicidal thoughts or plans secret.**

---

## What If They Say They Don't Want to Live?

Statements such as “I don't want to live anymore” should not be dismissed as attention-seeking or treated as something the person will automatically get over.

Remain calm and listen.

You can ask:

> “Are you thinking about killing yourself?”

If there is an immediate risk, seek urgent professional or emergency assistance. If it is safe to do so, stay with the person and involve additional support rather than trying to manage the situation entirely by yourself.

You do not need to become a crisis counselor. Your role is to **take the concern seriously, help connect the person with appropriate support, and prioritize immediate safety**.

---

## When Is Depression an Emergency?

Urgent help is appropriate when someone:

- Is in immediate danger
    
- Has an active suicide plan or intent
    
- Has seriously harmed themselves
    
- Cannot remain safe
    
- Is experiencing severe confusion, hallucinations, or another acute mental-health emergency
    
- May harm themselves or another person
    

Contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service in these situations.

If the person has already harmed themselves or there is an immediate risk of serious harm, do not rely solely on friends or family to manage the situation. Professional emergency support may be necessary.

---

## Can I Make Someone With Depression Get Better?

No. You cannot single-handedly cure someone else's depression.

But your support can still matter.

You may help by:

- Reducing isolation
    
- Listening without judgment
    
- Making everyday tasks easier
    
- Helping the person access professional care
    
- Encouraging them when motivation is low
    
- Reminding them that support is available
    
- Being a consistent source of connection
    

Recovery is not something you have to accomplish **for** someone. It is something you can support them through.

---

## What Professional Treatments May Help Depression?

Depression can be treated, and treatment varies according to the person's symptoms, preferences, health, and circumstances.

Evidence-based treatment options can include:

### Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy, sometimes called talk therapy or counseling, can help people understand and change patterns of thinking and behavior that contribute to depression. Different forms of psychotherapy may be used depending on the individual.

### Medication

Antidepressant medication may be prescribed by an appropriate healthcare professional when it is considered suitable.

A supporter should **not** tell someone to stop, start, or change prescribed medication. Medication decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare professional.

### Lifestyle and social support

Sleep routines, physical activity, regular meals, meaningful activities, and social connection can complement professional treatment. They should not be presented as a replacement for appropriate medical or psychological care.

## How Do I Help Someone With Depression Without Burning Myself Out?

Supporting someone you love can be emotionally exhausting.

You may experience worry, frustration, sadness, helplessness, or guilt. These feelings do not mean you do not care.

Remember:

**You can care deeply about someone without being available every moment.**

Protect your own wellbeing by:

- Setting realistic boundaries
    
- Maintaining your own sleep and routines
    
- Staying connected with your friends and family
    
- Taking regular breaks
    
- Continuing activities that matter to you
    
- Asking other trusted people to share practical responsibilities
    
- Seeking professional support for yourself when needed
    

You are a supporter, not someone's entire treatment system.

If you are becoming overwhelmed, it is reasonable to say:

> “I care about you and I want to support you, but I also need some time to rest. I'll check in with you tomorrow.”

Healthy boundaries can make support more sustainable.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. How do I help someone with depression?

Listen without judgment, stay connected, offer practical help, and encourage professional support when appropriate. You do not need to diagnose or fix the person. If there is a concern about suicide or immediate safety, seek urgent professional help.

## 2. What should I say to someone with depression?

Tell them you care, acknowledge that they are having a difficult time, and let them know you are available to listen. Ask what kind of support would be helpful instead of assuming you know what they need.

## 3. What should I not say to someone with depression?

Avoid minimizing or blaming statements such as “just think positive,” “snap out of it,” or “other people have it worse.” These comments can make someone feel misunderstood or ashamed.

## 4. How can I help a friend with depression?

Stay in contact, listen, offer specific practical help, invite them to low-pressure activities, and gently encourage professional support. Respect their autonomy unless there is an immediate safety concern.

## 5. How can I help someone with depression who doesn't want help?

Keep the conversation open, listen to their concerns, offer small forms of support, and check in again later. Avoid repeatedly pressuring them. If there is an immediate risk of self-harm or suicide, seek urgent help.

## 6. How do I encourage someone with depression to get help?

Ask whether they would be willing to talk with a healthcare or mental-health professional and offer practical assistance, such as finding a provider or going with them to an appointment. Encouragement is usually more helpful than force.

## 7. How can I support someone with depression every day?

Regular, manageable support can include checking in, helping with meals or errands, assisting with appointments, spending time together, and encouraging gentle routines. Ask what they find helpful.

## 8. What should I do if someone with depression talks about suicide?

Take them seriously. Ask directly about suicide and immediate safety, listen without judgment, and seek urgent professional or emergency support if necessary. If immediate danger exists, stay with them if it is safe and do not promise to keep suicidal plans secret.

## 9. Can I help someone with depression without being a therapist?

Yes. You can listen, provide companionship, offer practical assistance, and encourage professional care. However, you are not responsible for diagnosing or treating their depression.

## 10. How do I support someone with depression without burning out?

Set boundaries, maintain your own routines and relationships, take breaks, and ask others to share the responsibility of providing practical support. Caring for yourself is not abandoning the person you care about.

# A Simple Way to Remember How to Help

When you are unsure what to do, remember four things:

**Listen. Connect. Support. Encourage.**

**Listen** without immediately judging or solving.

**Connect** by continuing to check in, even when the person withdraws.

**Support** them with practical, manageable help.

**Encourage** professional care when it is appropriate—and seek urgent help when there is an immediate safety concern.

You do not have to have all the answers. Sometimes one of the most meaningful things you can offer is simply:

> “I'm here. We don't have to figure everything out today.”
